\begin{abstract}
Tuning a photoinjector requires trading off transverse emittances against energy spread while
keeping beam size, divergence, bunch length, energy and transmission within operational limits,
and each candidate setting must be evaluated with an expensive space-charge tracking simulation.
We study batch multi-objective Bayesian optimization (MOBO) for a six-parameter, three-objective,
seven-constraint design problem of a 200~MeV S-band electron injector simulated with ASTRA.
Independent Gaussian-process surrogates model the objectives and the constraint diagnostics, and
batches of candidates are selected with the noisy expected hypervolume improvement in its
logarithmic form (qLogNEHVI), either weighted by the modelled constraint satisfaction or
unconstrained. A weighted-sum Bayesian optimization baseline is included. The comparison is
paired and budget-matched: three algorithms over ten seeds, 160 simulations per run with a
shared quasi-random initial design per seed, failed simulations charged to the budget, and
hypervolume measured over feasible evaluated points against a fixed, predeclared reference.
Constraint-aware qLogNEHVI reached a median final feasible hypervolume of \pending{value [95\% CI]}
compared with \pending{value [CI]} for unconstrained qLogNEHVI and \pending{value [CI]} for the
weighted-sum baseline, with \pending{feasible fractions}. Representative Pareto-optimal settings
were re-simulated independently with hash-recorded inputs, \pending{N of 6} reproduced within
0.1\%, and their sensitivity to lattice jitter of $0.1\%$ in magnet strengths and $0.1^\circ$ in RF
phases was quantified. The software, configurations and complete campaign records are openly
available.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Bayesian optimization, multi-objective optimization, constrained
optimization, Gaussian processes, particle accelerators, photoinjector, reproducibility

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

High-brightness electron injectors determine the beam quality available to free-electron lasers
and ultrafast electron diffraction. Their performance is set in the first metres of the machine,
where space charge couples the transverse and longitudinal dynamics, so that the gun phase,
solenoid focusing, booster phasing and downstream quadrupole optics act jointly on the
emittances and the energy spread. These goals conflict, and the useful outcome of a design
study is a set of trade-offs rather than a single optimum. Each candidate must also satisfy
operational limits on beam size, divergence, bunch length, energy and transmission, and every
evaluation requires a space-charge tracking simulation that takes minutes.

Genetic algorithms such as NSGA-II~\cite{Deb2002} have long been used to map such trade-offs for
photoinjectors~\cite{Bazarov2005}, but they typically need tens of thousands of simulations;
neural-network surrogates reduce this cost once a large training set exists~\cite{Edelen2020}.
Bayesian optimization (BO)~\cite{Shahriari2016,Snoek2012} instead builds a probabilistic surrogate,
usually a Gaussian process (GP)~\cite{Rasmussen2006}, from the evaluations made so far and
selects new evaluations by maximizing an acquisition function. BO has become an established
tool for online and offline accelerator tuning~\cite{Duris2020,Roussel2021,Roussel2024}.
Multi-objective BO (MOBO) selects candidates by their expected improvement of the
hypervolume~\cite{Zitzler1999} of the observed Pareto front; the parallel, noise-aware variant
qNEHVI~\cite{Daulton2020,Daulton2021} and its numerically robust logarithmic form
qLogNEHVI~\cite{Ament2023} propose batches of candidates for parallel simulation. Constraints
can be handled by weighting the acquisition with the modelled probability of
feasibility~\cite{Gardner2014,Daulton2021}.

Reported comparisons of such methods on accelerator problems often rest on a single run, use
unequal simulation budgets, or measure hypervolume against reference points that move as the
search proceeds, which makes the claimed advantages hard to assess. Simulated optima are also
rarely re-simulated independently, although parallel execution of file-based simulation codes
can silently mix inputs and outputs. In earlier work we compared weighted-sum BO settings for the
injector studied here~\cite{Park2025ICABU}. In this paper we make three contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item We formulate the injector design as a three-objective, seven-channel constrained problem
  and solve it with constraint-aware qLogNEHVI, with independent GP surrogates for the objectives
  and for each constraint diagnostic (section~\ref{sec:methods}).
\item We compare constraint-aware qLogNEHVI, unconstrained qLogNEHVI and weighted-sum BO in a
  paired, budget-matched experiment over ten seeds, in which every simulation, including failed
  ones, is charged to the budget and hypervolume is measured over feasible evaluated points
  against a fixed reference declared before the experiment (section~\ref{sec:design}).
\item We audit selected Pareto-optimal settings by independent re-simulation with
  hash-recorded inputs and by their response to lattice jitter, and release the software and all
  campaign records (sections~\ref{sec:audit} and~\ref{sec:data}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Injector model and optimization problem}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Beamline and simulation}
The injector consists of an S-band RF photocathode gun, a gun solenoid, four 84-cell S-band
traveling-wave accelerating structures (ACC1--ACC4) and two quadrupoles, and is tracked with
ASTRA~v3.2~\cite{Floettmann2017Astra} through the \texttt{lume-astra} interface~\cite{LumeAstra}.
The structures start at $z = 1.810$, $5.610$, $9.411$ and $13.211$~m, and the quadrupoles
(length 65~mm) are located at $z = 8.700$ and $12.501$~m. Beam properties are evaluated at the
exit plane $z = 16.2$~m. Field maps of the gun, the solenoid and the structures are used, with
peak field amplitudes of $100$~MV/m in the gun and $22.26$~MV/m (ACC1, ACC2) and
$24.47$~MV/m (ACC3, ACC4) in the structures. ASTRA's automatic phasing is disabled, so RF phases
are absolute.

The initial distribution is a fixed file of $10^4$ macroparticles with a total charge of
$1.0$~nC. Space charge is computed on a cylindrically symmetric grid of $32\times32$ cells up to
$z = 5.25$~m and with a three-dimensional solver on a $32^3$ grid downstream, including the image
charge of the cathode. A circular aperture of radius 20~mm defines particle loss.

\subsection{Design variables, objectives and constraints}
The six design variables (table~\ref{tab:variables}) are the solenoid peak field $B_\mathrm{sol}$,
the two quadrupole gradients $G_{q1}$ and $G_{q2}$, the gun phase $\phi_\mathrm{gun}$ and two
common phases, $\phi_\mathrm{acc1/2}$ applied to ACC1 and ACC2 and $\phi_\mathrm{acc3/4}$ applied to
ACC3 and ACC4. Magnetic settings may vary by $\pm50\%$ and phases by $\pm10\%$ around the
nominal machine setting.

The three objectives, all minimized, are the normalized RMS emittances $\enx$ and $\eny$ and the
RMS energy spread $\sigma_E$ at the exit plane,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}) = \bigl(\enx(\mathbf{x}),\ \eny(\mathbf{x}),\ \sigma_E(\mathbf{x})\bigr).
\end{equation}
For reporting they are expressed in \umrad, \umrad\ and MeV. The surrogates work with the negated
values, because the acquisition functions are formulated for maximization.

A setting is \emph{feasible} if the seven diagnostics in table~\ref{tab:constraints} satisfy eight
inequalities; the mean kinetic energy is bounded from both sides. The thresholds are fixed before
the experiment and are not adjusted after observing algorithm performance.

\subsection{Simulation validity and failures}
\label{sec:failures}
Numerical validity and physical feasibility are kept separate. An evaluation is \emph{valid} if
ASTRA completes within its time limit (2000~s), its output can be parsed, all objectives and
diagnostics are finite, and the particle set reaches the exit plane to within 0.1~m; a beam that
is lost earlier is recorded as a premature loss. Invalid evaluations are never replaced by
artificial penalty values: they are excluded from surrogate training, recorded with their failure
category, charged to the evaluation budget and counted as infeasible. Failed simulations are not
retried, so every budget slot corresponds to exactly one simulation attempt.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Surrogate models}
Each objective is modelled by an independent GP~\cite{Rasmussen2006} with a constant mean and a
scaled Mat\'ern-5/2 kernel with automatic relevance determination over the six inputs, which are
normalized to the unit cube; outputs are standardized. The models are implemented in
BoTorch~\cite{Balandat2020} and GPyTorch~\cite{Gardner2018}, and the kernel hyperparameters are
fitted by maximizing the exact marginal likelihood. Because ASTRA is deterministic for a fixed
input, the observation noise is not inferred but fixed to
\begin{equation}
  \sigma^2_{\mathrm{obs},m} = \max\bigl(\eta\,\mathrm{Var}(y_m),\ \sigma^2_\mathrm{floor}\bigr),
  \qquad \eta = 10^{-6},\ \sigma^2_\mathrm{floor} = 10^{-24},
\end{equation}
for output $m$, which keeps the kernel matrices well conditioned without smoothing the data. For
constraint-aware optimization, each of the seven diagnostics is modelled by a further GP of the
same form, and all models are combined in one multi-output model with independent outputs.

\subsection{Acquisition functions}
\label{sec:acquisition}
Let $\mathcal{D}_n = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{y}_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ be the valid observations, with
$\mathbf{y}_i$ the negated objectives, and let $\mathbf{X} = (\mathbf{x}_1', \dots, \mathbf{x}_q')$
be a batch of $q$ candidates. The noisy expected hypervolume improvement
(qNEHVI)~\cite{Daulton2021} integrates the hypervolume improvement of the batch over the joint
posterior of the observed and the new points. We use its logarithmic form~\cite{Ament2023},
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_{q\text{LogNEHVI}}(\mathbf{X}) = \log\, \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\Bigl[
    \mathrm{HVI}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\xi}(\mathbf{X}) \mid \mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_n), \mathbf{r}_\mathrm{acq}\bigr)
    \prod_{k=1}^{8} s_\tau\bigl(-c_k(\boldsymbol{\xi})\bigr) \Bigr],
  \label{eq:qlognehvi}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ is a joint posterior sample, $\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_n)$ the Pareto
front of the sampled values at the observed points, and $\mathrm{HVI}$ the hypervolume improvement
over it with respect to the acquisition reference point $\mathbf{r}_\mathrm{acq}$. The expectation
is estimated with quasi-Monte-Carlo samples. In the constraint-aware variant, the improvement is
weighted by a smooth feasibility indicator: $c_k \le 0$ denotes satisfaction of the $k$-th
inequality of table~\ref{tab:constraints} evaluated on the sampled diagnostics, and $s_\tau$ is a
fat-tailed sigmoid with temperature $\tau = 10^{-3}$. \pending{Author decision before the
campaign: the residuals $c_k$ are currently passed in SI units, which makes $s_\tau$ nearly
uninformative for the beam-size, divergence and bunch-length channels (residuals
$\sim10^{-4}$) and a hard step for energy; normalizing each residual by its threshold would treat
the channels alike.} The unconstrained variant omits the product.

The weighted-sum baseline fits a single GP to the scalar cost
$g(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_m w_m f_m(\mathbf{x})/s_m$, where $s_m$ are the reporting scales
($1~\umrad$, $1~\umrad$, $1$~MeV) and $w_m = 1/3$, and selects batches with the logarithmic
noisy expected improvement (qLogNEI)~\cite{Ament2023}. It does not model the constraints.

\subsection{Acquisition optimization and reference points}
Batches are optimized jointly with multi-start L-BFGS-B~\cite{Byrd1995}: 1024 quasi-random
raw samples, 10 restarts and at most 200 iterations. If the optimizer fails or returns an invalid
batch, it is retried with a reduced budget, and as a last resort the batch is drawn from the
campaign's scrambled Sobol sequence~\cite{Owen1998}; every such fallback is recorded with the
proposal and reported. The acquisition reference point $\mathbf{r}_\mathrm{acq}$ is recomputed at
each iteration from the valid observations, offset from their worst values by 5\% of the observed
range. It steers the search only and is never used to report results.

Hypervolumes are reported against a fixed reference point declared before the experiment,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{r} = \bigl(1.5~\umrad,\ 1.5~\umrad,\ 1.5~\mathrm{MeV}\bigr),
\end{equation}
in units of \si{\micro\metre^2\,rad^2\,MeV}; the survey tables and figures of
section~\ref{sec:survey_results} use SI units, in which
$1~\si{\micro\metre^2\,rad^2\,MeV} = 10^{-6}~\si{\metre^2\,rad^2\,eV}$. The \emph{feasible hypervolume} after $n$ evaluations is
the hypervolume dominated by the feasible evaluated points (not by surrogate predictions) among
the first $n$; points that do not dominate $\mathbf{r}$ contribute nothing. Because the reference
is fixed and only evaluated points enter, the feasible hypervolume never decreases and is
comparable across algorithms, seeds and budgets.

\subsection{Execution and provenance}
Candidates of a batch are simulated in parallel worker processes, four at a time. Every attempt
runs in its own working directory, created from the template input, the field maps and the
initial distribution, and the SHA-256 hashes of all inputs, the ASTRA executable and the outputs
are stored with an attempt manifest. Evaluations are appended to a journal as they complete, and
the campaign state, including random-number generator states and pending proposals, is
checkpointed atomically after each batch, so that an interrupted campaign resumes without
repeating or losing evaluations. All computations use double precision on CPUs.

\section{Experimental design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Primary comparison}
The three algorithms---constraint-aware qLogNEHVI, unconstrained qLogNEHVI and weighted-sum
qLogNEI---were each run with ten seeds (42--51). Each run has a budget of 160 simulations: an
initial design of 16 points from a scrambled Sobol sequence~\cite{Owen1998}, followed by 18 batches
of $q = 8$. Within a seed, all algorithms start from the same initial design, so the runs of one
seed differ only in their acquisition strategy. All seeds are reported. The protocol (problem
definition, budgets, seeds, reference point and endpoints) was fixed before the experiment; pilot
and screening runs used to validate the pipeline are not part of the reported comparison.

\subsection{Endpoints and statistical analysis}
\label{sec:endpoints}
The primary endpoint is the final feasible hypervolume after 160 evaluations. Secondary endpoints
are the feasible hypervolume as a function of the number of evaluations, the fraction of feasible
evaluations (with all evaluations, including failures, in the denominator), the number of
evaluations until the first feasible point, the size of the feasible Pareto set, the failure
counts by category and the computational cost. For each algorithm we report the median over seeds
with a 95\% percentile bootstrap confidence interval~\cite{Efron1993} (1000 resamples with a fixed
generator seed). Because the initial designs are paired, the algorithms are compared through the
per-seed differences in final feasible hypervolume between constraint-aware qLogNEHVI and each
baseline, summarized by their median with a bootstrap interval and tested with the two-sided exact
Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{Wilcoxon1945} with Holm correction~\cite{Holm1979} for the two
comparisons. \pending{The paired-difference analysis is not yet produced by the pipeline.}

\subsection{Independent audit of operating points}
\label{sec:audit}
To check that the reported optima are reproducible and to assess their sensitivity to machine
errors, we audit the predeclared primary run of constraint-aware qLogNEHVI with seed~42. Six
representative points are taken from its feasible Pareto set: the minimizer of each objective;
the knee point, closest to the ideal point after min--max normalization of the front; the
balanced point, closest to the normalized front's centroid; and the point of largest crowding
distance~\cite{Deb2002}. A role may select the same setting as another.

Each representative is re-simulated in a fresh working directory. The rerun is \emph{verified} if
its feasibility agrees with the original and the largest relative difference over all objectives
and diagnostics is below 0.1\%, \emph{conditionally verified} below 10\%, and \emph{rejected}
otherwise. Both attempts are linked through their manifests, and a verification is withdrawn if
the recorded input hashes of either attempt are missing or do not match its archived inputs.

Robustness is evaluated with 50 perturbed simulations per representative, drawn with a fixed seed.
Magnet settings are perturbed by Gaussian relative errors with standard deviation 0.1\%, and the
gun and structure-pair phases by Gaussian errors of $0.1^\circ$. Fluctuations of the bunch charge,
laser spot size and pulse duration cannot be applied to the fixed initial distribution and are
therefore not included; the scores describe lattice jitter only. For each representative we
report the probability of feasibility $P_\mathrm{feas}$ (feasible perturbed simulations over all
perturbed simulations), the mean degradation
$D = \frac{1}{3}\sum_m \langle f_m\rangle / f_m^\mathrm{nom}$ and the smallest normalized
constraint margin $\mu_\mathrm{min}$ over channels and perturbations (positive inside the feasible
region), combined into the score
\begin{equation}
  S = P_\mathrm{feas}\, \frac{\max(0.5,\ 1 + \mu_\mathrm{min})}{\max(1,\ D)}.
\end{equation}
A representative with $P_\mathrm{feas} < 0.8$ is classified as fragile.

\subsection{Operating-point survey}
\label{sec:survey}
To illustrate the operating modes accessible to the injector, we additionally ran single campaigns
(seed 42; 16 initial points and 20 batches of 8, i.e.\ 176 simulations each) of the weighted-sum
method for five weightings $(w_x, w_y, w_E)$---balanced $(\frac13, \frac13, \frac13)$,
high brightness $(0.45, 0.45, 0.10)$, low energy spread $(0.10, 0.10, 0.80)$, horizontal
$(0.60, 0.20, 0.20)$ and vertical $(0.20, 0.60, 0.20)$---and one campaign of each qLogNEHVI
variant. The five weighted-sum runs together use five times the budget of a qLogNEHVI run, so the
survey is descriptive and is not used to compare algorithms. Its constraint-aware run was audited
with the same protocol.

The study has the following limitations. All results are simulations of one injector model with a
fixed initial distribution; source fluctuations were not perturbed, and agreement with the
machine has not been assessed. The space-charge model uses $10^4$ macroparticles and finite grids,
whose numerical resolution limits the accuracy of absolute emittance values, although all
algorithms are evaluated with the same model. Ten seeds give a first estimate of run-to-run
variability rather than a high-powered test. The constraint thresholds and the reference point
were fixed in advance; other choices may change absolute hypervolumes. Finally, the deterministic
noise model assumes that ASTRA returns identical results for identical inputs, which the
verification reruns test directly.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
We studied constraint-aware batch multi-objective Bayesian optimization for a six-parameter,
three-objective, seven-constraint design problem of a 200~MeV electron injector, in a paired,
budget-matched comparison over ten seeds with failure-inclusive accounting and a fixed hypervolume
reference. \pending{State the measured outcome of the primary comparison and of the audit.}
The software and the campaign records allow every number in this paper to be regenerated from the
recorded simulations. Future work will extend the robustness analysis to source fluctuations with
a validated distribution generator, distribute the simulations across compute nodes, and test the
optimized settings on the machine.
