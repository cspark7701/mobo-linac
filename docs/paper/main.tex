\title{Multi-objective Bayesian optimization of a 200~MeV injector-linac model derived from the Korea Light Source design}

\author{Chong Shik Park}
\email{kuphy@korea.ac.kr}
\affiliation{Department of Accelerator Science, Korea University, Sejong 30019, Republic of Korea}
\affiliation{Center for Accelerator Research, Korea University, Sejong 30019, Republic of Korea}

\begin{abstract}
\abstractspacing
Optimization of an electron injector linac requires balancing transverse beam emittances and
energy spread while satisfying beam transport and transmission constraints. We compare
Bayesian optimization pipelines on a fixed-lattice S-band injector-linac model derived from the
Korea Light Source design, using ASTRA space-charge tracking simulations. Magnetic strengths and
RF phases are varied to minimize the horizontal and vertical normalized RMS emittances and RMS
energy spread. Constraint-aware and unconstrained multi-objective Bayesian optimization are
compared with weighted-sum Bayesian optimization using matched simulation budgets and shared
initial designs. Feasibility is defined by a deliberately relaxed computational acceptance
profile; the stricter optimization requirements of the Korea Light Source technical design are
met by only a few sampled settings, with small diagnostic margins, and campaigns constrained
by them approach those limits to within the numerical resolution of the model. A
refinement check shows that the absolute emittances depend on the tracking resolution.
All methods identify feasible nondominated operating points, revealing trade-offs between
transverse beam quality and energy spread. Paired analyses support a higher feasible
sampling fraction for constraint-aware acquisition, which persists among energy-acceptable
settings and lies mainly in the transverse limits against unconstrained optimization but in
the energy band against weighted-sum optimization; all pipelines sample feasible settings more often
than a budget-matched space-filling search, while the weighted-sum implementation has the lowest
computational overhead.
Objective-quality comparisons depend on the indicator, the comparator and the fixed numerical
model. A survey of scalarization weightings explores additional trade-offs, and a limited
lattice-only jitter assessment retains feasibility for the selected operating points. The results demonstrate the use of
constraint-aware Bayesian optimization to identify feasible injector-linac settings and map
beam-quality trade-offs within the stated injector model and computational acceptance profile.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The Korea Light Source (KLS) is a fourth-generation storage-ring light-source project led by
the Korea Basic Science Institute, with a storage-ring energy of \SI{4}{\giga\electronvolt} and a circumference of
approximately \SI{800}{\metre}~\cite{KLSProjectOverview}. Earlier injector studies refer to the project as
Korea-4GSR~\cite{Kim2024KLSMOGA,Kim2025KLSError}. The injector chain uses an electron linac to
provide the beam for subsequent acceleration in the booster. The present work considers the
\SI{200}{\mega\electronvolt} S-band injector-linac reference model developed for that project and studies feasible
trade-offs among its exit-beam emittances and energy spread.

For storage-ring injection, beam emittance, energy spread and transport envelopes determine
compatibility with the transfer line and booster acceptance. The injector must deliver a
transmittable bunch with suitable phase-space properties rather than maximize a single beam
quality measure. In the low-energy section, RF fields and space charge shape transverse
emittance~\cite{Kim1989}; solenoid focusing and suitably phased accelerating structures can
compensate part of its growth~\cite{Carlsten1989,Serafini1997}. Magnetic strengths and RF phases
therefore act jointly on the transverse and longitudinal beam properties. A useful optimization
study should identify feasible trade-offs while retaining limits on sizes, divergences, bunch
length, mean energy and transmission. Here each proposed setting is evaluated with a
space-charge tracking simulation, and the production profile is compared explicitly with the
KLS technical design requirements.

Multi-objective genetic algorithms, in particular NSGA-II~\cite{Deb2002}, are the established tool
for mapping such trade-offs in photoinjector design~\cite{Bazarov2005,Hofler2013}. They have
contributed to injectors whose measured emittances approach the simulated
optimum~\cite{Gulliford2013}. Genetic algorithms, however, typically need tens of thousands of
simulations. Neural-network surrogates trained on large simulation sets reduce the cost of the
search itself~\cite{Edelen2020}. A recent study of the LCLS-II photoinjector compared genetic,
surrogate-based and scalarization methods and found that the outcome depends on the algorithm and
on its initial sample~\cite{Neveu2022}. Machine-learning methods are now explored throughout
accelerator design and operation~\cite{Edelen2018}.

Bayesian optimization (BO)~\cite{Jones1998,Snoek2012,Shahriari2016} targets problems in which
each evaluation is expensive. It fits a probabilistic surrogate, usually a Gaussian process
(GP)~\cite{Rasmussen2006}, to the observations accumulated so far and selects new evaluations
through an acquisition function that balances exploitation and exploration. Accelerator
applications include free-electron laser tuning at LCLS~\cite{McIntire2016,Duris2020}, safe
optimization at SwissFEL~\cite{Kirschner2019}, physics-informed GP priors~\cite{Hanuka2021},
and multi-objective online tuning~\cite{Roussel2021}; comparisons with reinforcement-learning
optimizers have also been reported~\cite{Kaiser2024}. These applications motivate using
probabilistic models to reduce the number of expensive evaluations, while the present study
addresses a simulation-based injector design problem with competing objectives and explicit
beam-quality limits. The wider accelerator BO literature is reviewed in ref.~\cite{Roussel2024}.

Multi-objective BO (MOBO) extends BO to the Pareto front. Early methods either scalarize the
objectives with random weights, as in ParEGO~\cite{Knowles2006}, or select candidates by their
expected improvement of the hypervolume dominated by the front~\cite{Zitzler1999,Emmerich2006}.
The parallel variants qEHVI and qNEHVI~\cite{Daulton2020,Daulton2021} propose batches of
candidates for simultaneous simulation and account for observation noise. Their logarithmic forms
avoid the vanishing gradients that hamper acquisition optimization~\cite{Ament2023}. Unknown
constraints can be handled with separate surrogates for the constrained quantities, which weight
the acquisition by the modelled probability of feasibility~\cite{Gardner2014,Gelbart2014,Daulton2021}.
This is the setting of an injector design. Scalarized BO, the simplest alternative, needs the
weights to be chosen in advance, and each weighting explores only part of the front.

The evidence that such methods outperform simpler alternatives on accelerator problems is often
thin. Comparisons can rest on a single run, on unequal simulation budgets, or on hypervolumes
measured against reference points that change during the search. In machine learning more broadly,
algorithm rankings have been shown to change with the random seed and the data
sampling~\cite{Henderson2018,Bouthillier2021}. Multiple paired runs and predeclared metrics are
therefore needed before a performance difference can be claimed. The sensitivity of simulated
operating points to machine errors also needs to be assessed,
because nominal feasibility alone does not establish tolerance to realistic perturbations.

Previous work on this injector established MOGA-based RF-cavity and beam-dynamics
optimization~\cite{Kim2024KLSMOGA} and examined operating and installation errors for selected
MOGA solutions~\cite{Kim2025KLSError}. A separate NAPAC publication compared scalarized BO
with MOGA using eight independent controls and a fractional energy-spread
objective~\cite{Park2025NAPAC}. Related work was presented orally at ICABU 2025~\cite{Park2025ICABU},
without an associated proceeding for this study. The present paper
focuses on MOBO, comparing its constraint-aware and unconstrained variants with scalarized BO.
The completed MOGA study provides published design context; a new MOGA benchmark is outside
this study's scope. Together, these studies motivate a comparison that separates the search
for feasible operating points from the selection of trade-offs within that feasible set. The
present work formulates the fixed-lattice injector problem with separate transverse-emittance
and energy-spread objectives and assesses selected operating points under lattice
perturbations. The objective is to characterize the empirical feasible Pareto
sets and the efficiency of feasible sampling, rather than to select a single weighted optimum.

The comparison combines objective-space views with diagnostic-space views so that an attractive
beam-quality trade-off can be traced to its constraint status. Feasible nondominated points are
identified jointly in the objective space, while measured sizes, divergences, bunch length,
energy and transmission determine whether a candidate enters that set. Repeated primary runs
separate common trends from dependence on the initial design, and the additional scalarization
survey illustrates how weighting choices sample different trade-offs. The lattice-jitter
assessment then examines the tolerance of representative settings under the stated perturbation
model. The earlier KLS studies provide a physical context for these results, with differences
in feasibility limits and error coverage made explicit in Sec.~\ref{sec:previous_kls}.

The contributions of this work are the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A constraint-aware comparison of multi-objective BO with two baselines, unconstrained
  MOBO and weighted-sum BO, on a space-charge-tracked injector with eight explicit
  beam-quality inequalities. The comparison uses ten paired runs per method with equal
  simulation budgets and shared initial designs, and counts failed simulations. The
  feasible fraction was predeclared; the predeclared fixed-reference hypervolume endpoint
  failed and is replaced by labelled post-hoc front-quality indicators
  (Secs.~\ref{sec:design}--\ref{sec:primary}).
\item An accounting of the feasible-sampling advantage of the constraint-aware pipeline
  by violated channel group. The advantage persists among evaluations that meet the energy
  band. Against unconstrained MOBO it lies mainly in transverse size and divergence
  violations, whereas against weighted-sum BO energy-band violations account for the larger
  share. Neither the effective step weight of the energy band nor the upper ACC3/ACC4 phase
  window explains it alone (Sec.~\ref{sec:attribution}).
\item A quantified reach of the KLS technical-design acceptance region. The primary
  evaluations are reclassified against the TDR inequalities; two marginal candidates and a
  MOGA-derived reference are checked under jitter; and three campaigns constrained directly
  by the TDR inequalities are run. At this budget and resolution, TDR-feasible observations
  are rare and lie within micrometres of the diagnostic limits, and their membership changes
  under numerical refinement (Secs.~\ref{sec:moga_reference} and~\ref{sec:tdr_reach}).
\item A space-filling reference for the same domain: the shared scrambled-Sobol initial
  designs and a budget-matched Sobol arm. It shows how much of the feasible-sampling gain
  any of the optimizers achieve over uniform sampling, and how much is specific to the
  constraint-aware pipeline (Sec.~\ref{sec:attribution}).
\end{enumerate}
These results describe the recorded pipelines on a fixed lattice at the nominal tracking
resolution, at which the emittances are not converged (Sec.~\ref{sec:problem}). They do not establish a unit-invariant acquisition, global optimality of the
reported fronts, or engineering acceptance of any setting.

\section{Injector model and optimization problem}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Beamline and simulation}
The reference model follows the earlier S-band injector studies~\cite{Kim2024KLSMOGA,Kim2025KLSError};
the engineering comparison uses the October 2025 Korea-4GSR Technical Design Report
(TDR)~\cite{KLSTDR2025}.
Figure~\ref{fig:layout} shows the fixed lattice used here, which consists of an S-band RF photocathode gun, a gun solenoid, four 84-cell S-band
traveling-wave accelerating structures (ACC1--ACC4) and two quadrupoles, and is tracked with
ASTRA~\cite{Floettmann2017Astra} through the \texttt{lume-astra} interface~\cite{LumeAstra}.
Beam properties are evaluated at the exit plane $z = \SI{16.2}{\metre}$. Field maps of the gun, the solenoid and the structures are used, with
peak field amplitudes of \SI{100}{\mega\volt\per\metre} in the gun and \SI{22.26}{\mega\volt\per\metre} (ACC1, ACC2) and
\SI{24.47}{\mega\volt\per\metre} (ACC3, ACC4) in the structures. ASTRA's automatic phasing is disabled, so RF phases
are absolute.

The initial distribution is a fixed \SI{1.0}{\nano\coulomb} file with \num{10000} records,
including the reference record; the evaluation journals count \num{9999} initial particles for transmission. Space charge is computed on a cylindrically symmetric grid of $32\times32$ cells up to
$z = \SI{5.25}{\metre}$ and with a three-dimensional solver on a $32^3$ grid downstream, including the image
charge of the cathode. A circular aperture of radius \SI{20}{\milli\metre} defines particle loss. The same source
file, field maps and space-charge settings are used for every algorithm, so the optimization
comparison does not mix changes in numerical fidelity with changes in candidate selection.
Keeping the initial distribution fixed also makes the present search a study of machine settings
for a specified source condition; the separate perturbation assessment changes lattice parameters
while retaining that source distribution.

The retained cathode file has six probe records in addition to the reference and ordinary
particles. Excluding the reference and probes for source characterization gives RMS transverse
sizes of \SI{0.483}{\milli\metre} in both planes and an RMS emission-time width of
\SI{2.551}{\pico\second}. The corresponding Gaussian-equivalent FWHM is
\SI{6.008}{\pico\second}; this conversion does not establish the distribution's functional
form. Ordinary-particle radii extend to \SI{0.9995}{\milli\metre}. Their transverse
momentum moments imply initial normalized RMS emittances of approximately
\SI{0.427}{\micro\metre\radian} in each plane, with nearly monoenergetic
\SI{0.600}{\electronvolt} initial kinetic energy. These are properties measured from the
fixed file, not a verified cathode-temperature model. The original generator recipe and
cathode material are not established by the retained input, so we do not equate this source
with the published study's laser specification merely because their durations are similar.
For the resolution check below, a generator input was reconstructed whose emission-time,
radius and momentum marginals reproduce those of the file; this does not establish the
original recipe.

The tracking resolution was adopted from the previous study of this injector by Kim et
al.~\cite{Kim2024KLSMOGA}, whose section 4.3 reports \num{10000} macroparticles, 32 radial and
at most 32 longitudinal space-charge cells, and mirror image charges. The present calculation
uses the same particle-count scale, cylindrical grid dimensions and image-charge treatment.
Its archived inputs additionally specify a Cartesian \(32^3\) grid and a solver transition at
\SI{5.25}{\metre}, which that published description does not state. No refinement
residual is quoted there, so we re-tracked ten reported settings with refined numerics: the
MOGA-derived nominal setting, the two TDR candidates (Table~\ref{tab:tdr_candidates}), the
closest observation of the TDR-constrained campaigns (Sec.~\ref{sec:tdr_reach}) and the six
representatives of Sec.~\ref{sec:settings}. Each factor was refined alone and then all
together: \num{20000} and \num{40000} particles from the reconstructed source, 48 and 64
cells in every space-charge grid dimension, and half and a quarter of the maximum and minimum
integration steps. The medium level combines the first refinement of each factor and the fine
level the second. Five independently seeded random sources of \num{10000} particles measure
the sampling spread. Reruns at the production settings reproduce the archived values to
floating-point precision, so the calculations are deterministic for a fixed distribution
(Supplement S1.13).

The emittances are not converged at the production resolution. At the fine level they are
lower by \SIrange{12}{34}{\percent} horizontally and \SIrange{17}{43}{\percent} vertically,
mainly through the integration step: quartering the step alone lowers them by up to
\SI{28}{\percent} and \SI{38}{\percent}. The fine values still differ from the medium ones by
up to \SI{9}{\percent}, so they are not converged either. Across the ten settings the order of
the horizontal and vertical emittances is largely but not fully kept (Spearman coefficients
0.79 and 0.94). The random sources spread the emittances by \SIrange{1}{12}{\percent} (standard
deviation). The energy spread changes by at most \SI{4}{\percent} and keeps its order, the
bunch length by at most \SI{1}{\percent}, the mean energy by at most
\SI{0.05}{\mega\electronvolt}, and the transmission not at all. Beam sizes and divergences fall by up to
\SI{22}{\percent}. Table~\ref{tab:resolution} summarizes these changes, separating the
fixed-source step refinement from the regenerated sources. A regenerated source reproduces the
marginals of the production file, not necessarily its multidimensional distribution, whereas
the step refinement uses the production file itself. All ten settings remain feasible under the
production profile at every level, whereas TDR classifications within a few percent of the
limits change (Sec.~\ref{sec:tdr_reach}). These ten settings are feasible and come mostly from
constraint-aware searches. They contain no rejected or baseline observation, so their stable
classification does not show that the classifications of the primary evaluations are stable;
Sec.~\ref{sec:primary} bounds that effect. All objective values, feasibility classifications and margins
are therefore results at the nominal resolution. The absolute emittances exceed the refined
values, and the method comparisons hold within this fixed numerical model. The fixed GP
observation variance provides regularization and does not measure tracking-discretization
uncertainty.

\subsection{Design variables, objectives and constraints}
The six design variables (Table~\ref{tab:variables}) are the solenoid peak field $B_\mathrm{sol}$,
the two quadrupole gradients $G_{q1}$ and $G_{q2}$, the gun phase $\phi_\mathrm{gun}$ and two
common phases, $\phi_\mathrm{acc1/2}$ applied to ACC1 and ACC2 and $\phi_\mathrm{acc3/4}$ applied to
ACC3 and ACC4. The nominal machine setting is a MOGA-derived operating point from the earlier
injector work, adapted to the present field amplitudes. Its ASTRA deck is identical to the
template used here, but it is not the tracked solution published in
ref.~\cite{Kim2024KLSMOGA}, which used a different gun field, cavity gradients, phase ranges
and constraints. Magnetic settings may vary by \SI{\pm50}{\percent} around this
nominal setting. The RF inputs are absolute wave phases at the start of tracking because automatic phasing is
switched off~\cite{Floettmann2017Astra}. They are not calibrated off-crest angles. The recorded
gun-phase interval is \SIrange{32.0625}{39.1875}{\degree}; the common ACC1/ACC2 phase spans
\SIrange{-43.4599}{-35.5581}{\degree}, and the common ACC3/ACC4 phase spans
\SIrange{279.0481}{341.0588}{\degree}. The respective nominal phases are
\SI{35.6250}{\degree}, \SI{-39.5090}{\degree} and \SI{310.0534}{\degree}.
Each structure pair receives one common phase, defining the six-control search problem.
These unequal angular windows were inherited from percentage offsets of the stored nominal
coordinates. They define the historical comparison rather than a demonstrated tolerance or an
origin-independent engineering prescription.

The drift lengths, RF-field amplitudes and field-map geometry remain fixed, so each candidate
represents a different focusing and phasing configuration of the same reference lattice. The
common phase variables are applied to their structure pairs together rather than treated as
independent controls, keeping the optimization dimension consistent with the chosen operating model.

The three objectives, all minimized, are the normalized RMS emittances $\enx$ and $\eny$ and the
projected total RMS energy spread $\sigma_E$ at the exit plane,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}) = \bigl(\enx(\mathbf{x}),\ \eny(\mathbf{x}),\ \sigma_E(\mathbf{x})\bigr).
\end{equation}
The energy-spread objective is the RMS energy column of ASTRA's longitudinal statistics,
converted from \si{\kilo\electronvolt} to \si{\electronvolt}; no longitudinal-correlation
subtraction or slice selection is applied. It therefore includes energy chirp. Reducing this
quantity can assist longitudinal matching, but compatibility with the booster also depends on
transport optics and acceptance, which are not simulated here.
For reporting they are expressed in \umrad, \umrad\ and \si{\mega\electronvolt}. The surrogates work with the negated
values, because the acquisition functions are formulated for maximization. An observation is
nondominated if no other observation is at least as good in every objective and strictly better
in at least one. This definition retains settings that improve one emittance or the energy
spread at the expense of another quantity, rather than combining those changes into a single
score. Consequently, coordinate-wise minima describe the ends of the trade-off set and need not
belong to the same accelerator setting.

A setting is \emph{feasible} within the present computational profile if the seven diagnostics
in Table~\ref{tab:constraints} satisfy eight inequalities:
\begin{align}
 \sigma_x&\le\SI{1}{\milli\metre}, &\sigma_y&\le\SI{1}{\milli\metre},\\
 \sigma_{x'}&\le\SI{1}{\milli\radian}, &\sigma_{y'}&\le\SI{1}{\milli\radian},\\
 \sigma_z&\le\SI{1}{\milli\metre}, &T&\ge0.90,\\
 E_\mathrm{kin}&\ge\SI{195}{\mega\electronvolt}, &E_\mathrm{kin}&\le\SI{205}{\mega\electronvolt}.
\end{align}
These limits define a deliberately relaxed computational acceptance profile, not an
engineering requirement of the KLS design. They were fixed in July 2026, before any of the
reported campaigns. Earlier studies of this injector used limits close to the TDR values
(\SI{0.3}{\milli\metre} sizes, \SI{0.3}{\milli\radian} divergences, \SI{99.99}{\percent}
transmission). In the present six-variable domain those limits produced a high rate of
infeasible early space-filling samples, and the production profile was adopted so that
feasible and infeasible observations both occur often enough for the algorithms to be compared. The TDR's
optimization requirements are given separately in Table~\ref{tab:constraints}; their source
is Table 2.1.4.6 of the report~\cite{KLSTDR2025}. The production transverse limits are about
three to four times looser, the transmission limit allows far larger losses, and the two-sided
energy interval differs from the TDR's lower bound. The completed campaigns therefore compare
algorithms on this computational profile, and TDR compatibility is assessed separately. This
is a fixed-lattice study with two quadrupole controls, rather than a model of the complete TDR
linac and downstream matching system. A valid
simulation that violates any limit is retained as an infeasible observation and cannot enter the
feasible Pareto set, even if its objective values are attractive. Conversely, passing a subset
of diagnostics is insufficient: the classification requires all inequalities to hold together.
This distinction is retained in the constraint-space plots, where the shaded regions apply only
to the displayed coordinates and marker fill communicates the joint constraint status.

\subsection{Simulation validity and failures}
\label{sec:failures}
Numerical validity and physical feasibility are kept separate. An evaluation is \emph{valid} if
ASTRA completes within its configured time limit, its output can be parsed, all objectives and
diagnostics are finite, and the particle set reaches the exit plane to within \SI{0.1}{\metre}; a beam that
is lost earlier is recorded as a premature loss. Invalid evaluations are never replaced by
artificial penalty values: they are excluded from surrogate training, recorded with their failure
category, charged to the evaluation budget and counted as infeasible. Failed simulations are not
retried, so every budget slot corresponds to exactly one simulation attempt.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Surrogate models}
Each objective is modelled by an independent GP~\cite{Rasmussen2006} with a constant mean and a
scaled Mat\'ern-5/2 kernel with automatic relevance determination over the six inputs, which are
normalized to the unit cube; outputs are centered and, when their observed variance permits, scaled. The models are implemented in
BoTorch~\cite{Balandat2020} and GPyTorch~\cite{Gardner2018}, and the kernel hyperparameters are
fitted by maximizing the exact marginal likelihood. Because ASTRA is deterministic for a fixed
input, the observation noise is not inferred but fixed to
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \sigma^2_{\mathrm{obs},m} &= \max\bigl(\eta\,\mathrm{Var}(y_m),\ \sigma^2_\mathrm{floor}\bigr),\\
  \eta &= 10^{-6},\qquad \sigma^2_\mathrm{floor} = 10^{-24},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
for output $m$. This regularizes the kernel matrices numerically. Because the effective
likelihood variance is nonzero, the posterior mean need not interpolate every observation
exactly. For constraint-aware optimization, each of the seven diagnostics is modelled by a
further GP of the same form, giving ten independent GPs; the energy diagnostic supplies both
energy inequalities. All models are combined in one multi-output model with independent
outputs. The effective likelihood floors, the handling of constant channels and the recorded
fitting outcomes are given in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.


\subsection{Acquisition functions}
\label{sec:acquisition}
Let $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ be a joint posterior draw at the observed baseline and
at the proposed batch $\mathbf{X}$. Define the feasible sampled baseline
$\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{feas}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_n)$ and the feasible candidate
subset $\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{X})$ by requiring all eight
inequalities at each individual point. The ideal constrained noisy
hypervolume-improvement criterion is
\begin{equation}
 \alpha_{\mathrm{ideal}}(\mathbf{X})=
 \log\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\!\left[
 \mathrm{HVI}\!\left(\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{X})\mid
 \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{feas}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_n),\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{acq}}\right)
 \right].
 \label{eq:qlognehvi}
\end{equation}
This expression defines the ideal target; the implementation uses BoTorch's
qLogNEHVI approximation~\cite{Daulton2021,Ament2023}. It evaluates batch
hypervolume contributions by inclusion--exclusion and applies feasibility
weights to each candidate participating in a contribution, rather than
multiplying the entire batch improvement by one feasibility factor. Sampled
baseline feasibility determines the baseline front. For each sampled candidate,
the weight is $\prod_{k=1}^{8}h(-c_k/\tau_c)$, where $h$ is BoTorch's
fat-tailed sigmoid and $\tau_c=10^{-3}$. The unconstrained variant omits the diagnostic
constraints. The remaining smoothing constants and the posterior sample counts are listed in
Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}. The separately available analytical Gaussian probability of
feasibility is a diagnostic; it is not substituted for these sampled weights.

The residuals are expressed in their recorded physical units: metres, radians,
electron-volts and transmission fraction, without threshold normalization.
Thus $\tau_c$ corresponds to \SI{1}{\milli\metre},
\SI{1}{\milli\radian}, \SI{0.001}{\electronvolt} and a transmission
fraction of 0.001 in their respective channels. These are scale parameters,
not equal physical transition widths. At a residual of zero the indicator is
0.5; at residuals $-\tau_c$ and $+\tau_c$ it is approximately 0.8089 and
0.1911, respectively. For a \SI{1}{\milli\metre} beam-size limit,
a size of zero therefore receives an indicator of 0.8089, while a size of
\SI{2}{\milli\metre} receives 0.1911. In the energy channel the same scale is
\SI{0.001}{\electronvolt}, negligible against the \SI{5}{\mega\electronvolt}
half-band, so the energy weight is effectively a step whose gradient vanishes
except within about a milli-electron-volt of either bound. This unit dependence
limits the interpretation of constraint weighting; no residual scaling is changed
when analysing the recorded campaigns. The constrained method compared below is
this raw-residual implementation, not a unit-invariant formulation.

The weighted-sum baseline fits a single GP to the scalar cost
$g(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_m w_m f_m(\mathbf{x})/s_m$, where $s_m$ are the reporting scales
(\SI{1}{\micro\metre\radian}, \SI{1}{\micro\metre\radian}, \SI{1}{\mega\electronvolt}) and $w_m = 1/3$, and selects batches with the logarithmic
noisy expected improvement (qLogNEI)~\cite{Ament2023}. The scales keep the weighted combination
from being determined simply by the different physical units of emittance and energy spread.
Equal weights define the balanced baseline used in the primary comparison, while the survey
changes those weights to explore other operating preferences. This method does not model the
constraints during candidate selection; the same physical feasibility checks and joint
nondominance calculation are nevertheless applied to its evaluated results, allowing a common
interpretation of the operating points found by all methods.

\subsection{Acquisition optimization}
Batches are optimized jointly with multi-start L-BFGS-B~\cite{Byrd1995}: 1,024 quasi-random
raw samples, 10 restarts and at most 200 iterations. If the optimizer fails or returns an invalid
batch, it is retried with a reduced budget, and as a last resort the batch is drawn from the
campaign's scrambled Sobol sequence~\cite{Owen1998}; every such fallback is recorded with the
proposal and reported. % C05 replacement for the acquisition-reference explanation.
The acquisition reference is recomputed before each MOBO proposal from all
valid observations, including physically infeasible observations. In negated
model coordinates it is
\begin{equation}
 r_{\mathrm{acq},m}=\min_i y_{im}
 -0.05\left(\max_i y_{im}-\min_i y_{im}+10^{-8}\right).
\end{equation}
The additive safeguard is applied numerically in each recorded output unit.
All 360 saved MOBO reference vectors agree with this rule applied to their
saved training sets. The noisy acquisition receives the complete valid
baseline before posterior-based pruning; recorded nominal feasibility is not
used to pre-filter that baseline. This adaptive reference steers acquisition
and remains distinct from the frozen reporting reference.

\section{Simulation study design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Primary comparison}
The three algorithms---constraint-aware qLogNEHVI, unconstrained qLogNEHVI and weighted-sum
qLogNEI---were each evaluated in ten independent runs with fixed seed numbers. Each run has a budget of 160 simulations:
an initial design of 16 points from a scrambled Sobol sequence~\cite{Owen1998}, followed by 18 batches
of $q = 8$. The three arms are specified optimization pipelines that share the physical
model, the initial design of each paired run and the simulation budget. They differ in the
optimization target, the number of fitted models, the use of constraint information and the
Monte Carlo sample counts, not only in an acquisition formula. Equal simulation budgets
therefore do not imply equal computation. The protocol (problem
definition, budgets, seeds, reference point and endpoints) was fixed before the simulation comparison; pilot
and screening runs used to validate the pipeline are not part of the reported comparison.

\subsection{Reported quantities and statistical summaries}
\label{sec:endpoints}
The comparison describes feasible nondominated observations and their trade-offs in physical
objective units. Feasible Pareto cardinality is computed jointly in all three objectives and
counts every feasible nondominated measured observation. Additional summaries are the fraction
of feasible evaluations (with all evaluations, including failures, in the denominator), the
number of evaluations until the first feasible point, invalid simulation counts and relative
computational cost. For each algorithm we report the median over runs with a \SI{95}{\percent} percentile
bootstrap confidence interval~\cite{Efron1993} (1,000 resamples with a fixed seed number).
These intervals summarize individual methods; exploratory paired inference is described below.
The limitation of the originally designated reporting endpoint is addressed in
Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}. Constraint-space figures use every numerically valid observation,
including physically infeasible settings, rather than only the Pareto subset. Their limits and
display units are read from each recorded configuration, and the recorded joint feasibility is
checked against those limits before plotting. Green shading marks the allowed interval or
rectangle in the displayed diagnostic coordinates; filled markers satisfy all constraints and
open markers violate at least one, potentially in another panel. Invalid simulations remain
counted in the campaign summary but receive no invented diagnostic coordinates. Individual-run
views and pooled views use the same definitions, with pooled views describing combined coverage
rather than the trajectory of a single campaign.

To assess objective-space quality beyond feasible sampling and set cardinality, we performed
an exploratory analysis of the archived per-run fronts. Objectives were divided by
\SI{1}{\micro\metre\radian}, \SI{1}{\micro\metre\radian} and \SI{1}{\mega\electronvolt}.
A common empirical reference set contained the 53 unique nondominated feasible observations
obtained after pooling all methods and runs. For a measured front $A$ and this reference $R$,
we evaluated the additive epsilon indicator~\cite{Zitzler2003Assessment} and
IGD+~\cite{Ishibuchi2015IGD}:
\begin{align}
I_{\epsilon+}(A,R)&=\max_{r\in R}\min_{a\in A}\max_j(a_j-r_j),\\
\mathrm{IGD}^{+}(A,R)&=|R|^{-1}\sum_{r\in R}\min_{a\in A}\|\max(a-r,0)\|_2,
\end{align}
where the maximum with zero is componentwise. Smaller values are preferred. Exact objective
duplicates were collapsed for set indicators. Empty measured fronts were assigned infinite
distance and zero dominated volume, retaining every evaluation in the budget.


Directional coverage $C(A,B)$ is the fraction of unique vectors in $B$ weakly dominated
by a vector in $A$; both directions are reported. We also examine the across-run probability
of attaining each target $r+\delta\mathbf{1}$, for $r\in R$ and normalized relaxations
$\delta\in\{0.05,0.10,0.25,0.50\}$. These are empirical probabilities of attaining specified
observed trade-offs, with no implied global-front guarantee. Quality histories are evaluated
at full-batch checkpoints, including initialization. Supporting plots and a separate post-hoc
reference-sensitivity analysis appear in Appendix~\ref{app:front_quality} (supplement items S1.2
and S1.3).

Exploratory inference used the ten matched groups as the sampling units. Paired bootstrap
intervals resampled complete groups jointly across methods, with 10,000 resamples.
We applied exact two-sided signed-rank tests~\cite{Wilcoxon1945} to four terminal endpoints---feasible fraction,
additive epsilon, IGD+ and the middle post-hoc hypervolume reference---and used Holm correction~\cite{Holm1979}
across all twelve pairwise comparisons. Zero differences were excluded from the signed ranks,
tied absolute differences received midranks, and all sign assignments were enumerated.
The tests assume independent groups and sign-symmetric differences under the null; the
pointwise bootstrap intervals are conditional on the fixed empirical reference and are not
multiplicity-adjusted. With ten groups the smallest exact two-sided signed-rank $p$-value is
$2/1024$, so the smallest Holm-adjusted value in this family is 0.0234; the paired effects and
their intervals are therefore the primary quantities. The feasible fraction needs no
objective-space reference. Epsilon, IGD+ and hypervolume, in contrast, are computed against
a reference estimated from the tested outcomes themselves. Their group differences share
that fitted quantity, so exact sign enumeration does not by itself establish the independent,
sign-symmetric null model, and Holm correction cannot repair an uncalibrated individual test
or post-hoc endpoint selection. We therefore treat the quality-indicator $p$-values as
exploratory, assumption-dependent calculations. As a sensitivity analysis, the matched groups
were also resampled 2,000 times with the empirical reference front and the hypervolume
reference rebuilt inside every resample. The resulting percentile intervals for the mean
paired differences include reference-construction uncertainty; no $p$-value is derived from
them.


The matched comparison contains constraint-aware qLogNEHVI, unconstrained qLogNEHVI
and equal-weight scalarized qLogNEI. The latter is a specific balanced scalarization,
not a claim to cover all weighted-sum strategies. A budget-matched Sobol arm, continuing
the shared initial design of each matched group, serves as a space-filling reference
(Sec.~\ref{sec:attribution}). No constraint-aware scalarized arm is included. Consequently
the results compare these implementations but do not isolate a benefit unique to
vector-valued acquisition over constraint-aware scalarization. The separate multiweight survey
has a larger aggregate budget and is not substituted for that missing control. Published MOGA results remain contextual rather than a new matched benchmark.

\subsection{Robustness assessment of operating points}
\label{sec:audit}
To assess sensitivity to machine errors, we select operating points from the predeclared
primary constraint-aware MOBO run. Six representative points are taken from its feasible Pareto set: the minimizer of each objective;
the ideal-distance point, closest to the ideal point after min--max normalization of the front
(a distance criterion, not a curvature-based knee); the
centroid-distance point, closest to the normalized front's centroid (named for its criterion,
not a physically balanced operating point); and the point of largest crowding
distance~\cite{Deb2002}. A role may select the same setting as another.

Robustness is evaluated with 50 perturbed simulations per representative, drawn with a fixed
seed number. Magnet settings receive independent Gaussian relative errors with standard
deviation \SI{0.1}{\percent}, while the gun and the two common structure-pair phases receive
independent Gaussian errors of \SI{0.1}{\degree}. Within each structure pair the two cavity
phase errors are identical, rather than independent. The same standardized draws are reused
across representatives (common random numbers), while their nominal parameter vectors differ.
Thus six sets of 50 trials produce 300 distinct perturbed settings, not 300 independent trials
of one design. The primary run is the first prescribed member of the primary campaign list,
not a run selected retrospectively for its robustness outcome; supplement item S1.1 retains
its exact identity. The survey assessment is recorded separately.

For each design we report the observed feasibility fraction and exact Clopper--Pearson
binomial limits~\cite{Clopper1934}, conditional on independent trials from the stated error model. Objective
changes are described by the mean ratio
$D = \frac{1}{3}\sum_m \langle f_m\rangle/f_m^{\mathrm{nom}}$, along with separate
objective means, population standard deviations and empirical fifth/ninety-fifth percentiles
in supplement item S1.10. Signed margins are $(u-y)/|u|$ for an upper threshold and
$(y-l)/|l|$ for a lower threshold. The reported $\mu_{\min}$ is the minimum over all
channels and trials. In particular, the two energy margins use \SI{195}{\mega\electronvolt}
and \SI{205}{\mega\electronvolt} as denominators, not the \SI{5}{\mega\electronvolt}
half-width of the acceptance band. Physical-unit margins are supplied alongside them.
This normalization is a reporting convention and does not define a probability or a
universal measure of tolerance across unlike channels.

The historical composite score is retained in the archived evidence, but is not used to rank
operating settings in the revised paper. Reporting its components avoids conflating sampled
feasibility, relative objective changes and threshold-normalized margins. Source fluctuations,
RF-amplitude errors and alignment errors are outside this assessment. No error-amplitude
scan or comparative robustness superiority over the baseline algorithms is inferred from
these small-jitter tests; expanded error models would be needed for those claims.

\subsection{Operating-point survey}
\label{sec:survey}
To illustrate the operating modes accessible to the injector, we additionally ran single campaigns
(16 initial points and 20 batches of 8, i.e.\ 176 simulations each) of the weighted-sum
method for five weightings $(w_x, w_y, w_E)$---equal weight $(\frac13, \frac13, \frac13)$,
emittance emphasis $(0.45, 0.45, 0.10)$, spread emphasis $(0.10, 0.10, 0.80)$, horizontal
emphasis $(0.60, 0.20, 0.20)$ and vertical emphasis $(0.20, 0.60, 0.20)$---and one campaign of each qLogNEHVI
variant. The campaigns use fixed seed numbers; scalarization weight cases have independently
generated initial designs, whereas the two MOBO runs share an initial design.
The five weighted-sum runs together use five times the budget of a qLogNEHVI run, so the
survey is descriptive and is not used to compare algorithms. Its constraint-aware run was assessed under lattice jitter
with the same protocol.

\subsection{Simulation validity and computational comparison}
All 4,800 simulations of the primary comparison were valid; no simulation-invalid failure was

used CPU worker processes with at most eight concurrent simulations.
All 540 post-initialization

Internal optimizer warnings and fitting recovery are retained in the run logs.
For each paired run, campaign duration was divided by that of scalarized BO with the same
initialization. The median ratios are 2.74 for constraint-aware and 1.89 for unconstrained
MOBO. These are measured costs of the recorded implementations and adaptive trajectories,
not intrinsic algorithmic complexity or a GPU-versus-CPU speedup. Campaigns ran sequentially,
with eight evaluation workers per campaign; hardware, software and input identities are
retained in supplement item S1.1.

Outside the union of recorded evaluation intervals lies a median \SI{71.4}{\percent},
\SI{57.0}{\percent} and \SI{19.2}{\percent} of campaign time for constraint-aware,

\subsection{Sources of the feasible-sampling difference}
\label{sec:attribution}
Two properties of the recorded setup could produce a feasible-sampling advantage without
balancing all eight inequalities: the effective step weight of the energy band
(Sec.~\ref{sec:acquisition}) and the upper quarter of the ACC3/ACC4 window, in which
every sampled observation of every method lies outside the energy band. We therefore
classified each post-initialization evaluation (evaluations 17--160) of the primary runs into
one exclusive category of violated channel groups: energy band, transverse size or
divergence, and bunch length. Transmission was never violated. Because the feasible fraction
equals one minus the sum of the infeasible category shares, each paired feasible-fraction
difference divides exactly among these categories. This is an accounting of the observed
gaps, not a causal identification of acquisition terms. The analysis is descriptive and uses
only the archived evaluations.

The upper ACC3/ACC4 quarter contributes, but is not the main source of the difference.
Constraint-aware MOBO placed a median \SI{15.3}{\percent} of its post-initialization
evaluations there, compared with \SI{22.6}{\percent} and \SI{24.7}{\percent} for the
unconstrained and scalarized pipelines (Fig.~\ref{fig:attribution}a). Because every evaluation
there is infeasible, the lower mean occupancy (by 0.060 and 0.094) corresponds to about a fifth
and a third of the respective gaps. Relative to
unconstrained MOBO, the mean paired gap of 0.276 (\SI{95}{\percent} interval 0.229--0.325)
comes mostly from transverse-only violations, which account for \SI{62}{\percent} of it; the
categories involving the energy band account for \SI{34}{\percent}. Relative to scalarized
BO, whose weighted cost ignores both groups, the gap of 0.311 (0.277--0.347) is shared: energy
band violations, alone or together with transverse ones, account for \SI{63}{\percent} and
transverse-only violations for \SI{31}{\percent}. Bunch-length-only violations are slightly
more frequent for the constraint-aware pipeline in both comparisons
(Fig.~\ref{fig:attribution}b).

Among evaluations that satisfy the energy band, the median fraction meeting all remaining
inequalities was 0.578 for constraint-aware MOBO, compared with 0.254 and 0.231 for the
baselines. The mean paired differences were 0.328 (0.268--0.389) and 0.342 (0.293--0.392),
positive in all ten matched groups for each comparison. These conditional fractions compare
populations that each method selected, so they do not isolate an energy-independent
mechanism. They do show that the feasible-sampling advantage of the recorded constrained
pipeline is not confined to the energy boundary: among energy-acceptable evaluations it meets
the transverse size and divergence limits more often, even though those channels receive the
softest weights. This attribution describes the executed
raw-residual pipeline on the recorded domain. It does not predict the behaviour of a
normalized-residual method or of a campaign in a restricted phase window.

A budget-matched Sobol arm provides a space-filling reference for the same domain. In each
of ten runs the scrambled Sobol sequence of the matched group was continued to 160 points
without a surrogate or an acquisition function, so its first 16 points are that group's
shared initial design. Only 41 of its 1,440 post-initialization points are feasible
(\SI{2.8}{\percent}), consistent with the 5 of 160 shared initial points (\SI{3.1}{\percent}).
Points within one scrambled sequence are not independent, so the sampling unit is the run: the
ten run-level fractions range from \SI{1.4}{\percent} to \SI{4.9}{\percent} (standard deviation
1.3 percentage points; bootstrap interval over the ten runs \SIrange{2.1}{3.6}{\percent}, which
is approximate with ten replicates). Every pipeline exceeds the Sobol arm in all ten matched
groups. The mean paired differences in post-initialization feasible
fraction are 0.418 (0.384--0.453) for constraint-aware MOBO, 0.142 (0.115--0.172) for
unconstrained MOBO and 0.107 (0.088--0.127) for scalarized BO. The constraint-blind pipelines
therefore sample feasible settings several times more often than space-filling sampling, but
they account for at most about one third of the constraint-aware gain over the Sobol arm
(shares of 0.34 and 0.26). The
same channel classification shows why. Transverse violations fall from \SI{95.3}{\percent} of
the Sobol points to \SI{76.0}{\percent} for both baselines, and bunch-length violations from
\SI{55.1}{\percent} to \SIrange{32}{33}{\percent}. Energy-band violations change less and in
opposite directions: they fall from \SI{39.0}{\percent} to \SI{32.2}{\percent} for
unconstrained MOBO (paired difference $-0.069$, $-0.098$ to $-0.041$) and rise slightly to
\SI{42.6}{\percent} for scalarized BO ($+0.035$, 0.017--0.052).
Minimizing emittance and energy spread thus favours smaller transverse sizes, divergences and
bunch lengths, whereas nothing in the baselines' targets steers the mean energy. The Sobol
arm's terminal feasible fronts are also farther from the unchanged empirical reference, with
median epsilon 1.45 and IGD+ 1.14 (post hoc). Every pipeline improves on it in both indicators,
by mean paired differences between $-0.74$ and $-1.07$ in epsilon and between $-0.75$ and
$-0.94$ in IGD+, with intervals excluding zero. None of its feasible points dominates a
reference point.

\subsection{Offline sensitivity of constraint smoothing}
To quantify the unit dependence of the recorded constraint weights, we evaluated single-point
qLogNEHVI on 32 fixed Sobol candidates using frozen early, middle and late surrogate snapshots
from the prescribed constraint-aware run. Each evaluation used 128 common posterior samples
and the same full baseline without pruning, isolating residual scaling from changes in
baseline selection. This diagnostic does not replay the production eight-point optimizer.
Changing the diagnostic representation from metres/radians/electron-volts to
millimetres/milliradians/mega-electron-volts while retaining the raw numerical temperature
changed candidate ranks: Spearman correlations with the original ranking were
0.793, 0.852 and 0.880, with three or four of the original top five candidates retained.

As a unit-invariant sensitivity convention, we divided size, divergence and length residuals
by their upper thresholds, energy residuals by the \SI{5}{\mega\electronvolt} half-band,
and transmission residuals by the allowed loss fraction of 0.1. These scales express distance
relative to the stated production acceptance profile; they are not tuned engineering
uncertainties. At dimensionless temperature 0.01, equivalent unit representations agreed in
log acquisition value within $1.5\times10^{-13}$. Temperatures 0.001 and 0.1 were also
examined as sensitivity settings. The highest-ranked probe candidate remained unchanged in
all cases, while lower ranks varied. No normalized-residual campaign was executed, so these
calculations establish unit dependence and sensitivity, not improved feasible sampling or
beam quality for a corrected method. The probe also differs from production in using single
candidates rather than eight-point batches, no baseline pruning and one run, so an unchanged
top-ranked probe does not show that the production optimizer is invariant to residual units.
The three-pipeline comparison measures the executed implementation; this diagnostic only
shows that the implementation's constraint weighting depends on the chosen units.

\subsection{Predictive checks on unseen batches}
\label{sec:predictive_checks}
We restored all 540 saved pre-proposal GP snapshots and predicted their next eight observations,
verifying that none of these observations appeared in the corresponding training identities.
This chronological check uses \num{1440} post-initialization observations per method and
requires no refitting. Gaussian observation intervals include the saved effective fixed
likelihood variance. Their variance is taken from the posterior covariance diagonal before
any absolute floor imposed by the distribution's variance accessor. The latter can inflate
small-unit uncertainties; both conventions are recorded in supplement item S1.8, but the
covariance-based convention is used here. No production model or proposal is changed.

Nominal \SI{95}{\percent} interval coverage for constraint-aware MOBO is
\SI{78.6}{\percent}, \SI{79.7}{\percent} and \SI{88.8}{\percent} for horizontal
emittance, vertical emittance and energy spread. The corresponding unconstrained values
are \SI{78.1}{\percent}, \SI{81.1}{\percent} and \SI{87.1}{\percent}; the scalarized
cost has \SI{81.3}{\percent} coverage. Thus the saved Gaussian uncertainties are generally
too narrow on the sampled next-batch trajectories. These method-specific adaptive inputs
are not a common independent test set, so predictive errors do not establish a ranking
of surrogate architectures.

For diagnostic observations near a production boundary, coverage ranges from approximately
\SI{80.2}{\percent} to \SI{89.2}{\percent}. The post-hoc neighborhood is within
\SI{10}{\percent} of each absolute threshold, except energy, for which it is within
\SI{0.5}{\mega\electronvolt} of either bound. Counts and physical-unit errors are supplied
in Appendix~\ref{app:predictive}. Transmission is constant; perfect prediction of its
observed value cannot validate discrimination of losses.

Three hypotheses could explain the undercoverage; none is isolated here. First, the likelihood variance is
fixed at $\eta=10^{-6}$ of the training variance, so the intervals contain almost no
irreducible term and depend entirely on the posterior variance of a stationary kernel.
Second, a single Mat\'ern-5/2 length scale per control cannot represent both the smooth
interior response and the sharp changes near phase-dependent energy loss or beam-size
growth. Third, the test points are not random: each batch is chosen where the acquisition
expects improvement or feasibility, often at the edge of the sampled region. Because the
constraint weights use the same Gaussian predictions, overconfident constraint
predictions make the feasibility weights too sharp, both towards one and towards zero.
They change which candidates are proposed. They do not affect the reported diagnostics,
which are tracked values.

The whole-trajectory RMSE combines all stages and both regions. For constraint-aware MOBO,
the horizontal-emittance coverage is \SI{89.9}{\percent} for production-feasible next-batch
observations and \SI{69.5}{\percent} for infeasible ones, with RMSE of
\SI{2.60}{\micro\metre\radian} and \SI{10.33}{\micro\metre\radian}. The corresponding
values for the unconstrained method are \SI{92.7}{\percent} and \SI{75.0}{\percent}. For
the transverse diagnostics, coverage rises from \SIrange{75.6}{80.0}{\percent} in batches
1--6 to \SIrange{88.3}{91.0}{\percent} in batches 13--18, and for energy from
\SI{67.5}{\percent} to \SI{97.5}{\percent}. Bunch length is the exception: its coverage
falls from \SI{89.2}{\percent} to \SI{84.4}{\percent}, and is \SI{73.5}{\percent} for late
infeasible observations. The
emittance coverage does not improve monotonically with stage
(\SI{74.0}{\percent}, \SI{83.3}{\percent} and \SI{78.5}{\percent}). In the late stage, the
horizontal-emittance RMSE for feasible observations is \SI{0.98}{\micro\metre\radian}. The
surrogates are therefore least reliable away from the feasible set, where infeasible
proposals are most common. Even near the feasible region, the intervals remain below their
nominal coverage, so no claim of calibrated local prediction is made.

To test the first cause, we refitted every archived pre-proposal training set offline with
the production surrogate builder twice. One fit used the production fixed noise and the
other used homoscedastic noise inferred by marginal likelihood. The chronological
next-batch check was then repeated. The fixed-noise refits reproduce the coverage of the
saved snapshots to within \num{0.5} percentage points. Inferred noise raises the
constraint-aware coverage to \SI{83.4}{\percent}, \SI{86.6}{\percent} and
\SI{92.7}{\percent} for the three objectives. The diagnostic coverage rises to
\SIrange{86.9}{93.9}{\percent}, and the RMSE changes by at most \SI{14}{\percent},
except for energy. For energy, the RMSE falls from \SI{0.89}{\mega\electronvolt} to
\SI{0.14}{\mega\electronvolt}. The fitted noise is \numrange{e-4}{e-3} of the training
variance. For energy and bunch length, it lies at the lower bound of the fitting
constraint, so the energy improvement reflects regularization of a nearly
interpolating fit, not detected simulation noise. Inferred noise thus removes part,
but not all, of the undercoverage, which shows that the coverage is sensitive to the
regularization. Kernel misspecification and adaptive test points remain hypotheses for the
remainder; this check does not test them. The production campaigns are unchanged.
In \num{88} of \num{4680} refits, both optimizers failed and the hyperparameters were
restored to their initial values, as in production. These are not equivalent fits, and the
refit records do not identify them, so the reported refit coverage includes them.

\subsection{Interpretation of selected settings}
\label{sec:settings}
Appendix~\ref{app:settings} gives the complete controls and objectives of the six distinct
settings selected from the primary constraint-aware front. The horizontal-emittance extreme
has \SI{2.8061}{\micro\metre\radian} horizontal and \SI{6.8492}{\micro\metre\radian}
vertical emittance, whereas the energy-spread extreme trades larger, nearly equal emittances
for \SI{0.36286}{\mega\electronvolt} spread. These roles describe different compromises
within one selected front; they are not six independently optimized operational modes. The
nominal setting itself was re-evaluated separately and is compared with these fronts in
Sec.~\ref{sec:moga_reference}.

The quadrupole centers are at \SI{8.70046}{\metre} and \SI{12.50092}{\metre}, each with
\SI{0.065}{\metre} effective length. They lie between ACC2/ACC3 and ACC3/ACC4,
respectively, rather than after the complete linac. For the horizontal-emittance extreme,
the saved normalized emittances are nearly equal near \SI{5.2}{\metre}
(\SI{3.2985}{\micro\metre\radian} and \SI{3.3001}{\micro\metre\radian}), but are
\SI{2.9298}{\micro\metre\radian} and \SI{4.1003}{\micro\metre\radian} near
\SI{12.4}{\metre}. The initially similar source moments and growing downstream asymmetry
are consistent with differing transport through the quadrupole lattice. They do not isolate
quadrupole chromaticity, solenoid coupling, space charge or numerical effects as the cause;
ideal uncoupled linear focusing alone is not an emittance-growth explanation. Such causal
attribution would require controlled diagnostic calculations and is not claimed here.

The energy-spread objective is the projected RMS spread of the full tracked bunch from
ASTRA's longitudinal moment output, including correlated chirp. It is neither a slice spread
nor a dechirped value. The horizontal-emittance extreme has a longitudinal position--energy
correlation of approximately $-0.968$ at the exit, showing that correlation contributes to
its spread. RF phases and upstream focusing vary together across selected points, so their
trade-offs cannot be attributed to a single phase control from these observations alone.
Finally, transmission is unity throughout the primary sample, and the largest mean energy is
\SI{201.41}{\mega\electronvolt}, below the production upper bound. These two inequalities
are unviolated in the observed sample, while each other inequality is violated at least once;
this does not remove any required diagnostic or establish inactivity throughout the domain.

\subsection{Robustness to lattice jitter}
\label{sec:robustness_results}
Table~\ref{tab:robustness_summary} reports the six distinct settings from the selected
primary constraint-aware run. Each retained feasibility in all 50 trials. For each design,
the exact two-sided \SI{95}{\percent} feasibility interval is $[0.9289,1]$; the corresponding
one-sided \SI{95}{\percent} upper bound on failure probability is \SI{5.82}{\percent}.
These bounds are design-specific and not simultaneous guarantees. Common random numbers
also preclude interpreting the combined count as independent tests of a universal reliability.
The survey's six settings likewise each passed their 50 trials and have the same individual
binomial bounds under their stated error model.

For the primary settings, $D$ ranges from 0.988 to 1.064. This average conceals differences
between objectives: at the horizontal extreme, the mean horizontal and vertical emittances
increase by approximately \SI{2.73}{\percent} and \SI{6.61}{\percent}, while mean energy
spread changes by less than \SI{0.01}{\percent}. At the crowding-distance point, the vertical
mean increases by approximately \SI{14.74}{\percent}. The smallest threshold-normalized
margin, 0.01595 at the lower energy bound, corresponds to a physical headroom of
\SI{3.11}{\mega\electronvolt}, or 0.622 of the energy half-band; it is not a
\SI{1.595}{\percent} probability of failure. Numerical refinement changes the mean energy of
the reported settings by at most \SI{0.05}{\mega\electronvolt} and their bunch length by at
most \SI{1}{\percent} (Sec.~\ref{sec:problem}), small against this headroom, and every
representative remains feasible under the production profile at all refinement levels. Other primary settings are limited in this
normalization by bunch length or the upper energy bound. All classifications refer to the
original production profile, not certification against all TDR specifications, and are
conditional on the simulated lattice-only error model; success in every trial is not a
reliability guarantee.

Values of $D$ below one indicate that perturbations improved the objectives on average. For
the spread extreme and the centroid-distance point, 14 and 8 of the 50 perturbed settings,
all feasible, Pareto-dominate their nominal setting; the ideal-distance point and the vertical
extreme have 2 and 1 such trials, and the other two settings none. The selected settings are
therefore not locally converged along the jitter directions. Small control changes can still
improve all three objectives, as expected after a finite search of 160 evaluations.

The jitter model omits RF-amplitude errors. A conditional, linearized estimate indicates their
effect on the mean energy only. It takes the energy gains of the re-evaluated MOGA-derived
nominal tracking (Sec.~\ref{sec:moga_reference}): \SI{4.1}{\mega\electronvolt} from the gun and
\SI{93.3}{\mega\electronvolt} and \SI{102.8}{\mega\electronvolt} from the two structure
pairs. It assumes that each gain scales linearly with its amplitude at fixed phase. A
\SI{0.2}{\percent} RMS amplitude error, the RF-gradient error of the JKPS model, then shifts the
mean energy by \SI{0.28}{\mega\electronvolt} RMS if the pairs vary independently and by
\SI{0.40}{\mega\electronvolt} if all amplitudes vary together. This is about ten times the
\SIrange{0.014}{0.046}{\mega\electronvolt} RMS energy variation produced by the simulated phase
jitter, and about an eighth of the smallest lower-energy headroom of the representatives,
\SI{3.11}{\mega\electronvolt}. The estimate is not a bound. It does not cover the bunch-length
and upper-energy margins that limit other representatives, nor the changes in phase slippage,
focusing, chirp and transverse diagnostics that an amplitude change also produces, so it does
not establish that the representatives stay feasible under amplitude jitter.

The two TDR-passing observations of Table~\ref{tab:tdr_candidates} were assessed with the same
50-trial protocol and classified against the TDR inequalities. Both remained
production-feasible in every trial, but only 36 and 44 of the 50 perturbed settings met the TDR
inequalities (exact \SI{95}{\percent} intervals 0.58--0.84 and 0.76--0.95). The failures were
mainly of the horizontal-size limit and, for candidate B, also the vertical divergence. Under
the archived fragility threshold of 0.8, candidate A would be classified as fragile. Their TDR
lower-energy margins, \SI{0.31}{\mega\electronvolt} and \SI{0.14}{\mega\electronvolt}, are
comparable to the linearized amplitude-induced shift of
\SIrange{0.28}{0.40}{\mega\electronvolt} RMS, which indicates that amplitude jitter would cause
further TDR failures. These marginal settings therefore do not provide jitter-tolerant TDR
operation in this model.

\subsection{The MOGA-derived nominal setting as a reference point}
\label{sec:moga_reference}
The nominal setting at the centre of the search domain was re-evaluated once with the
production pipeline. The run reproduced the values previously recorded for the same deck,
with $(\enx,\eny,\sigma_E)=(\SI{3.4728}{\micro\metre\radian},\
\SI{3.2968}{\micro\metre\radian},\ \SI{0.39419}{\mega\electronvolt})$ at
\SI{200.19}{\mega\electronvolt} and full transmission. It satisfies all eight production
inequalities and the NAPAC profile. Against the TDR optimization requirements it fails only the
vertical divergence, with \SI{0.2690}{\milli\radian} against \SI{0.2663}{\milli\radian}.

No feasible observation of any primary run Pareto-dominates this setting. The searches found
feasible settings with smaller horizontal emittance (247, 40 and 42 observations for the
constraint-aware, unconstrained and scalarized pipelines), smaller vertical emittance (65, 8
and 9) or smaller energy spread (67, 32 and 10), but none improved all three objectives
simultaneously. The additive epsilon by which a run's feasible front must be relaxed to weakly
dominate the setting had medians of 0.039, 0.195 and 0.134 in the normalized units, with
ranges 0.003--0.088, 0.025--0.634 and 0.024--0.293. The constraint-aware fronts therefore came
closest to this reference, but within 160 evaluations no primary feasible observation
Pareto-dominated it at the nominal resolution. The empirical fronts contain improvements in
individual objectives and map trade-offs around the earlier operating point; the reference is
not thereby established as an optimum.

Under the same lattice-jitter protocol as the representatives, all 50 perturbed settings
satisfied the production profile (exact \SI{95}{\percent} interval 0.929--1). The mean
objective ratio was $D=1.030$, with mean emittances of 3.60 and
\SI{3.47}{\micro\metre\radian} and a mean energy spread of \SI{0.3948}{\mega\electronvolt}. The
smallest normalized margin was at the upper energy bound. Twelve of the 50 perturbed settings
met the TDR optimization inequalities (0.131--0.382), mostly failing the vertical divergence or
horizontal size limits. The nominal setting therefore lies close to the TDR boundary. This is a
single reference from a differently posed earlier optimization; it is not a MOGA benchmark and
supports no statement about MOGA sample efficiency.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Constraint-aware acquisition produced a larger feasible fraction and a larger feasible Pareto
set over ten runs, while completing campaigns more slowly than the unconstrained and
weighted-sum methods. The paired analysis supports its feasible-sampling advantage over both
baselines without any estimated reference. Evidence for objective quality is weaker in kind:
the paired epsilon, IGD+ and hypervolume differences favour the constraint-aware pipeline even
when the reference is rebuilt in each resample, but they are measured against a reference
estimated from the same runs, and the exploratory signed-rank calculations against
unconstrained MOBO do not pass the multiplicity correction. They also hold only for the fixed
source file at the production resolution, whereas the feasible-sampling advantage persists
under the bounding shifts of the refinement check. A larger
feasible set therefore should not be read as uniform superiority of the observed Pareto front.
The feasible-sampling advantage is not confined to the sharply weighted energy band or to the
infeasible upper ACC3/ACC4 phase region, although both contribute: it persists among
energy-acceptable evaluations. Against unconstrained MOBO it lies mainly in the transverse size
and divergence violations, and against weighted-sum BO mainly in the energy band. No primary
feasible observation Pareto-dominates the MOGA-derived nominal setting at the centre of the
search domain at the nominal resolution, although many improve on it in individual objectives;
the reported fronts therefore describe trade-offs around that earlier operating point. All methods identified feasible nondominated settings,
with the objective projections exposing the trade-offs between transverse emittance and energy spread.
The sampled lattice-jitter tests identify several operating points that remain feasible within
the simulated tolerance model; this does not establish performance on the real machine.

\subsection{Relation to previous KLS injector studies}
\label{sec:previous_kls}
Kim et al.\ optimized RF-cavity geometry and injector beam dynamics with
MOGA~\cite{Kim2024KLSMOGA}. Their abstract reports approximately
\SI{3.79}{\micro\metre\radian} horizontal normalized emittance and
\SI{0.30737}{\mega\electronvolt} energy spread. These summary values differ from the
specific tracked setting in section 4.3, which gives horizontal and vertical normalized
emittances of \SI{3.9586}{\micro\metre\radian} and \SI{3.9424}{\micro\metre\radian},
and \SI{0.165}{\percent} RMS energy spread at \SI{201.79}{\mega\electronvolt}
(approximately \SI{0.3330}{\mega\electronvolt}). The distinction matters when comparing
individual operating points. The present primary ideal-distance point has
$(\enx,\eny,\sigma_E)=(\SI{2.9983}{\micro\metre\radian},\
\SI{3.5207}{\micro\metre\radian},\ \SI{0.4034}{\mega\electronvolt})$.
It exchanges lower transverse emittances for higher energy spread relative to that tracked
example. Different acceptance limits and settings prevent attributing this exchange solely to
the algorithm. The earlier source was specified by laser parameters, whereas the present study
reuses one fixed distribution file; equality of their full initial distributions is not established.

The NAPAC comparison~\cite{Park2025NAPAC} used independently varied cavity phases and
normalized fractional energy spread, while this study uses paired cavity controls and absolute
RMS energy spread. Its feasible-region and weighting conventions also differ. Consequently,
those published results supply prior-work context rather than a matched-budget control for
this study. No new MOGA/NSGA-II campaign or reevaluation of its published setting is included,
and no MOGA speedup or superiority is inferred from the present comparisons. The only MOGA-derived
quantity used here is the nominal setting of Sec.~\ref{sec:moga_reference}, which the earlier
work adapted to the present field amplitudes; it is a reference point, not the output of a
matched MOGA search.

The subsequent JKPS study~\cite{Kim2025KLSError} reports nominal energy spreads of
\SIrange{0.34145}{0.48248}{\mega\electronvolt} for its selected cases. Its tabulated emittances are geometric, whereas
ours are normalized, so those emittance values are not directly compared. Its beam-size and
bunch-length limits were \SI{0.2}{\milli\metre} and \SI{0.9}{\milli\metre}, respectively, compared with \SI{1.0}{\milli\metre} for both here;
JKPS also imposed a \SI{0.5}{\mega\electronvolt} energy-spread limit, whereas here energy spread is an objective
without an upper feasibility cutoff. Feasibility therefore has different meanings across the studies.

The JKPS operating-error model used RF-gradient and phase standard deviations of \SI{0.2}{\percent} and
\SI{0.2}{\degree}, with \SI{0.1}{\percent} magnet-strength errors. It also studied \SI{100}{\micro\metre}
position errors and \SI{0.4}{\milli\radian} rotations, using 1,000 samples per stage with a Gaussian distribution
truncated at two standard deviations. Our tests use \SI{0.1}{\degree} RF-phase and \SI{0.1}{\percent} magnet-strength
errors with 50 samples per representative. They omit tracked RF-gradient errors, whose effect on the
mean energy alone is estimated by linearization in Sec.~\ref{sec:robustness_results}, and
alignment errors. Thus the
observed feasibility under lattice jitter complements the earlier tolerance study; it does not
show greater robustness under its broader error model. The earlier study identified alignment
errors as a principal sensitivity, supporting their inclusion in future assessments.

\subsection{Reach of the TDR acceptance region}
\label{sec:tdr_reach}
The archived evidence shows that TDR-feasible observations are rare and close to the diagnostic
limits. Only two of the 4,800 primary evaluations meet the TDR inequalities, both with
micrometre-scale size and bunch-length margins (Table~\ref{tab:tdr_candidates}). The
MOGA-derived nominal setting misses them only in vertical divergence, by
\SI{2.7}{\micro\radian}, and about a quarter of its jittered neighbours pass. Closeness to a
diagnostic limit is not closeness to a boundary of the control domain: in normalized controls,
candidate~A lies between 0.48 and 0.86 in every coordinate, and candidate~B is interior except
for a gun phase of 0.96. Two points do not locate a feasible region in six dimensions. The
six controls can therefore produce TDR-feasible settings, but the production-profile campaigns
were not aimed at them.

Three further constraint-aware campaigns targeted it directly. They used the TDR optimization
inequalities as constraints, with the primary protocol and the initial designs of the first
three matched groups. None of their 480 evaluations met the TDR inequalities. The closest
observation of each campaign missed by \SI{4.2}{\percent} in vertical divergence, by
\SI{0.4}{\percent} in both horizontal size and vertical divergence (about
\SI{1.3}{\micro\metre} and \SI{1.1}{\micro\radian}), and by \SI{3.6}{\percent} in horizontal
size. The production-profile constraint-aware runs of the same groups contain candidate~A and
otherwise miss by 2.3 and \SI{9.4}{\percent}. After initialization, the horizontal-size limit
had the worst relative margin in \SI{62}{\percent} of the targeted campaigns' evaluations and
vertical divergence in \SI{24}{\percent}. Each campaign still produced 52--58
production-feasible observations. Targeting the TDR inequalities therefore did not increase TDR
coverage at this budget: the limits are approached to within a few micrometres and
microradians, but not met. Three finite-budget campaigns at one resolution do not show that the
region is inaccessible to these controls.

These classifications lie within the numerical resolution of the model
(Sec.~\ref{sec:problem}). Candidate~B meets the TDR inequalities at all fifteen refinement
and source levels. Candidate~A meets them at the medium and fine levels but fails in
horizontal size on the 64-cell grid and with two of the five random sources. Conversely, the
MOGA-derived nominal setting and the closest observation of the TDR-constrained campaigns meet
them at the medium and fine levels. Each fails with four of the five random sources, the
nominal setting also with every single-factor refinement and the campaign observation with
both particle-count refinements. Whether a setting within a few percent of the TDR limits meets
them is therefore not resolved by the production calculation. The statement that the
TDR-constrained campaigns did not enter the region holds only at that resolution. The TDR
describes a longer linac with further matching quadrupoles and adjustable RF amplitudes. Those
are held fixed or absent here; they are design options for future studies, and the present
model can show neither that they are needed nor how much they would add.

\subsection{Limitations}
The feasible fronts are empirical sets from finite searches in the recorded domain. They
characterize a broader production acceptance profile than the TDR optimization requirements,
and do not establish global Pareto optimality or full injection acceptance. The comparison is
restricted to the performed constraint-aware, unconstrained and equal-weight scalarized
implementations. No matched constrained-scalarized arm was evaluated, and the unequal-budget
weighting survey cannot replace that control. The Sobol arm is a space-filling reference for
the recorded domain, not a tuned global optimizer. Ten paired runs
characterize initialization variability for this problem; they do not establish universal
algorithm superiority. The original constraint smoothing is unit-dependent, and the offline
normalization probes supply no physical-performance evidence for a corrected method.

The tracking model uses one fixed source file and the particle, grid and step resolution of
the previous injector study. The refinement check of Sec.~\ref{sec:problem} shows that the
emittances are not converged at this resolution and that TDR classifications near the limits
change under refinement, so all results are nominal-resolution results. Reclassifying the
archived evaluations under bounding shifts preserves the feasible-sampling comparison, but the
shifts come from ten settings and do not model refinement at every evaluation. Whether the
front-quality comparison would change at a converged resolution has not been tested. Small margins in particular are not interpreted as numerically
resolved: the two TDR-passing observations clear the horizontal-size limit by
about 2.5 and \SI{8.9}{\micro\metre} and the bunch-length limit by about 8.2 and
\SI{8.0}{\micro\metre}. Likewise, the selected settings are not presented as numerically
validated engineering settings, and objective differences of order
\SI{0.1}{\micro\metre\radian} between individual settings are not claimed to be resolved. The
original source-generation recipe and cathode model are also not established by the retained file.
Near-symmetric source moments and downstream trajectory changes support a transport
interpretation of the transverse asymmetry, but do not isolate its causal mechanism. The
lattice-jitter results exclude source, RF-gradient and alignment errors and cannot be directly
compared with the broader JKPS error model or interpreted as machine certification.

Physical crest calibration and an engineering basis for the inherited unequal phase windows
remain unavailable. Earlier JINST phases are relative to maximum energy gain, whereas the
current inputs are absolute wave phases. The upper quarter of the ACC3/ACC4 interval,
\SIrange{325.5561}{341.0588}{\degree}, contains 259, 345 and 394 sampled observations for
constraint-aware, unconstrained and scalarized BO, respectively, all outside the energy band.
Because upstream controls vary jointly, this does not establish a causal phase cutoff or
predict rankings after changing the domain. Next-batch predictive checks also show
undercoverage of the saved Gaussian intervals, concentrated in the infeasible region and
only partly removed by inferred noise. Estimated model feasibility must therefore not be
interpreted as calibrated machine reliability; physical feasibility is determined by the
tracking diagnostics themselves.

The originally designated fixed-reference hypervolume endpoint was zero for every campaign
because its reference $(\SI{1.5}{\micro\metre\radian},\ \SI{1.5}{\micro\metre\radian},\
\SI{1.5}{\mega\electronvolt})$ had emittance components below the observed values. This does
not indicate absence of feasible solutions. The replacement quality analyses are explicitly
post hoc, with data-derived references and sensitivity checks; they do not retrospectively
validate the original endpoint. Their empirical reference remains sampling-dependent, and
jointly nondominated points can appear dominated in a two-dimensional projection. No
comparison with accelerator measurements is made in this simulation study.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
We compared three specified Bayesian optimization pipelines on a six-control, three-objective
injector-linac model derived from the KLS design, with seven diagnostic channels and eight
inequalities, in a paired, budget-matched study of ten runs with failure-inclusive accounting.
All simulations were valid, and every pipeline found feasible nondominated settings that expose
the trade-offs between transverse emittance and energy spread.

The best-supported result is a feasible-sampling advantage of the constraint-aware pipeline
under the stated computational profile. It holds in every matched group without any estimated
reference and persists among energy-acceptable evaluations. Against unconstrained MOBO it lies
mainly in the transverse size and divergence limits, and against weighted-sum BO mainly in the
energy band. All three pipelines sample feasible settings more often than a
budget-matched Sobol search, but the constraint-blind pipelines account for at most about one
third of the constraint-aware gain over that search. Indicators of front quality also favour the constraint-aware
pipeline, including when the empirical reference is rebuilt in each resample, but they rest
on a reference estimated from the same runs and do not establish uniform superiority or global
optimality. No primary feasible observation Pareto-dominates the MOGA-derived nominal setting
at the centre of the domain at the nominal resolution, although many improve on it in
individual objectives.

The engineering conclusion is limited. The production profile is deliberately looser than the
KLS TDR optimization requirements, under which only two marginal primary observations are
acceptable and the MOGA-derived nominal setting misses narrowly. Campaigns that took the TDR
inequalities as constraints came within a few micrometres of them but found no acceptable
setting at the production resolution. Their closest observation and the MOGA-derived nominal
setting meet them at the combined medium and fine refinement levels but fail with four of five
regenerated sources, so membership near the limits is not numerically resolved. TDR-feasible
settings are therefore rare and marginal in this six-control model at this budget. A design
study aimed at TDR acceptance would need those requirements as its constraints; the additional
matching and RF controls of the full TDR linac are options for such a study, not a
demonstrated remedy. Lattice-jitter tests of the selected settings
retained feasibility under the simulated lattice-only error model, which is not a reliability
guarantee. All values are nominal-resolution results. A refinement check of ten reported
settings showed that the emittances are not converged at that resolution, whereas at those
ten settings the energy spread, bunch length, mean energy, transmission and production-profile
classifications are stable; absolute emittances should not be quoted without a converged
calculation. Under the corresponding bounding shifts the feasible-sampling advantage of the
constraint-aware pipeline persists in every matched group. The archived
runs and analysis scripts support regeneration of all reported values; Supplement S1 lists the
inputs that each requires. Future work will repeat the
comparison at a converged tracking resolution, extend the robustness analysis to source
fluctuations with a validated distribution generator, distribute the simulations across compute
nodes, and test the optimized settings on the machine.

\section{Executed implementation details}
\label{app:implementation}
This appendix records implementation details of the executed pipelines that matter for
reproduction but not for the physical argument.

\paragraph{Supporting package.} Supplement S1 is the versioned \texttt{mobo\_linac}~1.0.0
repository snapshot supplied with this manuscript for review and otherwise available from the
author on request; it is identified by the commit hash recorded in the review package. Its
evidence map
(\path{docs/paper/supplement/README.md}) assigns item numbers S1.1--S1.15 to the reported
results and links each to its evidence file and generating script. Every production step was
started from a recorded clean commit. The primary benchmark used \texttt{8ad6c2c}, the
operating-point survey \texttt{239c466}, and the candidate audit and lattice jitter
\texttt{f6ce000}. The revision simulations began at \texttt{bfb0be0}. The
revision-analysis scripts are distinct from this production code. They read the completed
run ledgers and verify their checksums, and they do not modify run directories. The
configuration's development benchmark defaults were overridden on the command line. These
overrides, the ledger checksums and the configuration hash are listed in item S1.1. The
complete run directories (about \SI{44}{\giga\byte}) are not distributed with S1 and are
available from the author. The evidence files do not all need the same inputs. Those computed
from the evaluation ledgers alone, about \SI{10}{\mega\byte} identified by checksum, regenerate
from that minimal set, as tested for representative cases. Others also need run records, saved
GP checkpoints, retained tracking outputs or the re-tracking records; S1 lists the inputs of
every item. Sampler base draws were not archived, so proposals cannot be replayed bitwise.

\paragraph{Surrogate noise and fitting.} The fixed-noise expression of
Sec.~\ref{sec:methods} specifies the requested variance in each output's recorded units. The
outcome transform divides it by the square of the observed standard deviation, and the float64
likelihood additionally enforces a minimum variance of $10^{-6}$ in the transformed
coordinates. Outputs with observed standard deviation below $10^{-8}$ are centered without
variance scaling. Transmission is constant in the primary data: its requested variance is
$10^{-24}$, its transform scale is one, and its saved effective likelihood variance is
$10^{-6}$. Thus not every channel has unit-variance transformed observations, and the
requested physical variance need not equal the effective likelihood variance. Across the saved
primary models, 1,714 constraint-aware channel fits completed with L-BFGS and 86 used Adam
recovery; the corresponding counts were 535 and five for unconstrained MOBO, and 180 and zero
for scalarized BO. No saved fit used a restored-prior fallback. These are fitting outcomes
rather than simulation failures or candidate-proposal fallbacks.

\paragraph{Acquisition smoothing and sampling.} Besides the constraint temperature $\tau_c$,
qLogNEHVI smooths positive improvements and extrema in log space with
$\tau_{\mathrm{ReLU}}=10^{-6}$ and $\tau_{\max}=10^{-3}$; scalarized qLogNEI uses
$\tau_{\max}=10^{-2}$ and $\tau_{\mathrm{ReLU}}=10^{-6}$. The executed call sites use baseline
pruning and BoTorch's default normal Sobol samplers, with 128 posterior samples for qLogNEHVI
and 512 for scalarized qLogNEI. These counts and constants are reconstructed from the call
sites and the matching archived BoTorch version, because acquisition objects and sampler base
draws were not saved; bitwise replay of the proposals is therefore not possible. The model
dimension and baseline sizes remain below the Sobol engine's dimension limit. The posterior
sample counts differ from the 1,024 raw candidate-initialization samples.

\paragraph{Campaign time.} To avoid double-counting concurrent workers, the union of recorded
evaluation start and end intervals was formed for each campaign. The remainder of campaign
time combines model fitting, acquisition optimization, scheduling, setup and export; the logs
do not support a separate measured allocation to each component. Summed worker runtimes are
retained for reproducibility but are not interpreted as elapsed fractions.
