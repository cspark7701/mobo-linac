\title{Multi-objective Bayesian optimization of a \SI{200}{\mega\electronvolt} electron injector linac for the Korea Light Source}

\author{Chong Shik Park}
\affiliation{Department of Accelerator Science, Korea University,\\
Sejong 30019, Republic of Korea}
\affiliation{Center for Accelerator Research, Korea University,\\
Sejong 30019, Republic of Korea}
\emailAdd{kuphy@korea.ac.kr}

\abstract{
Optimization of an electron injector linac requires balancing transverse beam emittances and
energy spread while satisfying beam transport and transmission constraints. We investigate
Bayesian optimization of the S-band injector-linac model developed for the Korea Light Source
using ASTRA space-charge tracking simulations. Magnetic strengths and RF phases are varied to
minimize the horizontal and vertical normalized RMS emittances and RMS energy spread.
Constraint-aware and unconstrained multi-objective Bayesian optimization are compared with
weighted-sum Bayesian optimization using matched simulation budgets and shared initial designs.
All methods identify feasible nondominated operating points, revealing trade-offs between
transverse beam quality and energy spread. Paired analyses support a higher feasible
sampling fraction for constraint-aware acquisition, while weighted-sum optimization completes campaigns faster.
Objective-quality comparisons depend on the indicator and comparator. A survey of
scalarization weightings explores additional operating modes, and sampled lattice perturbations
preserve feasibility for the selected operating points. The results demonstrate the use of
constraint-aware Bayesian optimization to identify feasible injector-linac settings and map
beam-quality trade-offs for the Korea Light Source.}

\keywords{Beam dynamics, Accelerator modelling and simulations, Multi-Objective Bayesian Optimization}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The Korea Light Source (KLS) is a fourth-generation storage-ring light-source project led by
the Korea Basic Science Institute, with a storage-ring energy of \SI{4}{\giga\electronvolt} and a circumference of
approximately \SI{800}{\metre}~\cite{KLSProjectOverview}. Earlier injector studies refer to the project as
Korea-4GSR~\cite{Kim2024KLSMOGA,Kim2025KLSError}. The injector chain uses an electron linac to
provide the beam for subsequent acceleration in the booster. The present work considers the
\SI{200}{\mega\electronvolt} S-band injector-linac reference model developed for that project and studies feasible
trade-offs among its exit-beam emittances and energy spread.

The electron injector sets the brightness available to the rest of a linear accelerator. X-ray
free-electron lasers~\cite{Emma2010,Kang2017} and mega-electron-volt ultrafast electron
diffraction~\cite{Weathersby2015} need bunches with small transverse emittance and small energy
spread. Both are fixed largely in the first metres of the machine, where the beam is still
non-relativistic and space charge dominates. There, the RF fields of the gun and the space-charge
forces both increase the emittance~\cite{Kim1989}. Solenoid focusing combined with suitably phased
booster structures can compensate part of this growth~\cite{Carlsten1989,Serafini1997}. As a
result, the gun phase, the solenoid strength, the booster phasing and the downstream quadrupole
optics act jointly on the emittances and the energy spread. These goals conflict, so a design
study should deliver the set of achievable trade-offs, the Pareto front, rather than one optimum.
Each candidate must also respect operational limits on beam size, divergence, bunch length,
energy and transmission. Each candidate setting is evaluated with a space-charge tracking simulation.

Multi-objective genetic algorithms, in particular NSGA-II~\cite{Deb2002}, are the established tool
for mapping such trade-offs in photoinjector design~\cite{Bazarov2005,Hofler2013}. They have
contributed to injectors whose measured emittances approach the simulated
optimum~\cite{Gulliford2013}. Genetic algorithms, however, typically need tens of thousands of
simulations. Neural-network surrogates trained on large simulation sets reduce the cost of the
search itself~\cite{Edelen2020}. A recent study of the LCLS-II photoinjector compared genetic,
surrogate-based and scalarization methods and found that the outcome depends on the algorithm and
on its initial sample~\cite{Neveu2022}. Machine-learning methods are now explored throughout
accelerator design and operation~\cite{Edelen2018}.

Bayesian optimization (BO)~\cite{Jones1998,Snoek2012,Shahriari2016} targets problems in which
each evaluation is expensive. It fits a probabilistic surrogate, usually a Gaussian process
(GP)~\cite{Rasmussen2006}, to the observations accumulated so far and selects new evaluations
through an acquisition function that balances exploitation and exploration. Accelerator
applications include free-electron laser tuning at LCLS~\cite{McIntire2016,Duris2020}, safe
optimization at SwissFEL~\cite{Kirschner2019}, physics-informed GP priors~\cite{Hanuka2021},
and multi-objective online tuning~\cite{Roussel2021}; comparisons with reinforcement-learning
optimizers have also been reported~\cite{Kaiser2024}. These applications motivate using
probabilistic models to reduce the number of expensive evaluations, while the present study
addresses a simulation-based injector design problem with competing objectives and explicit
beam-quality limits. The wider accelerator BO literature is reviewed in ref.~\cite{Roussel2024}.

Multi-objective BO (MOBO) extends BO to the Pareto front. Early methods either scalarize the
objectives with random weights, as in ParEGO~\cite{Knowles2006}, or select candidates by their
expected improvement of the hypervolume dominated by the front~\cite{Zitzler1999,Emmerich2006}.
The parallel variants qEHVI and qNEHVI~\cite{Daulton2020,Daulton2021} propose batches of
candidates for simultaneous simulation and account for observation noise. Their logarithmic forms
avoid the vanishing gradients that hamper acquisition optimization~\cite{Ament2023}. Unknown
constraints can be handled with separate surrogates for the constrained quantities, which weight
the acquisition by the modelled probability of feasibility~\cite{Gardner2014,Gelbart2014,Daulton2021}.
This is the setting of an injector design. Scalarized BO, the simplest alternative, needs the
weights to be chosen in advance, and each weighting explores only part of the front.

The evidence that such methods outperform simpler alternatives on accelerator problems is often
thin. Comparisons can rest on a single run, on unequal simulation budgets, or on hypervolumes
measured against reference points that change during the search. In machine learning more broadly,
algorithm rankings have been shown to change with the random seed and the data
sampling~\cite{Henderson2018,Bouthillier2021}. Multiple paired runs and predeclared metrics are
therefore needed before a performance difference can be claimed. The sensitivity of simulated
operating points to machine errors also needs to be assessed,
because nominal feasibility alone does not establish tolerance to realistic perturbations.

Previous work on this injector established MOGA-based RF-cavity and beam-dynamics
optimization~\cite{Kim2024KLSMOGA} and examined operating and installation errors for selected
MOGA solutions~\cite{Kim2025KLSError}. A separate NAPAC publication compared scalarized BO
with MOGA using eight independent controls and a fractional energy-spread
objective~\cite{Park2025NAPAC}; ICABU contributions were presentations. The present paper
focuses on MOBO, comparing its constraint-aware and unconstrained variants with scalarized BO.
The completed MOGA study provides published design context; a new MOGA benchmark is outside
this study's scope. Together, these studies motivate a comparison that separates the search
for feasible operating points from the selection of trade-offs within that feasible set. The
present work formulates the fixed-lattice injector problem with separate transverse-emittance
and energy-spread objectives, compares constraint-aware and unconstrained MOBO with scalarized
BO under matched budgets and paired initial designs, and assesses selected operating points
under lattice perturbations. The objective is to characterize the empirical feasible Pareto
sets and the efficiency of feasible sampling, rather than to select a single weighted optimum.

The comparison combines objective-space views with diagnostic-space views so that an attractive
beam-quality trade-off can be traced to its constraint status. Feasible nondominated points are
identified jointly in the objective space, while measured sizes, divergences, bunch length,
energy and transmission determine whether a candidate enters that set. Repeated primary runs
separate common trends from dependence on the initial design, and the additional scalarization
survey illustrates how weighting choices sample different operating modes. The lattice-jitter
assessment then examines the tolerance of representative settings under the stated perturbation
model. The earlier KLS studies provide a physical context for these results, with differences
in feasibility limits and error coverage made explicit in section~\ref{sec:previous_kls}.

\section{Injector model and optimization problem}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Beamline and simulation}
The reference model follows the earlier S-band injector studies~\cite{Kim2024KLSMOGA,Kim2025KLSError};
the engineering comparison uses the October 2025 Korea-4GSR Technical Design Report
(TDR)~\cite{KLSTDR2025}.
Figure~\ref{fig:layout} shows the fixed lattice used here, which consists of an S-band RF photocathode gun, a gun solenoid, four 84-cell S-band
traveling-wave accelerating structures (ACC1--ACC4) and two quadrupoles, and is tracked with
ASTRA~\cite{Floettmann2017Astra} through the \texttt{lume-astra} interface~\cite{LumeAstra}.
Beam properties are evaluated at the exit plane $z = \SI{16.2}{\metre}$. Field maps of the gun, the solenoid and the structures are used, with
peak field amplitudes of \SI{100}{\mega\volt\per\metre} in the gun and \SI{22.26}{\mega\volt\per\metre} (ACC1, ACC2) and
\SI{24.47}{\mega\volt\per\metre} (ACC3, ACC4) in the structures. ASTRA's automatic phasing is disabled, so RF phases
are absolute.

The initial distribution is a fixed \SI{1.0}{\nano\coulomb} file with \num{10000} records,
including the reference record; the evaluation journals count \num{9999} initial particles for transmission. Space charge is computed on a cylindrically symmetric grid of $32\times32$ cells up to
$z = \SI{5.25}{\metre}$ and with a three-dimensional solver on a $32^3$ grid downstream, including the image
charge of the cathode. A circular aperture of radius \SI{20}{\milli\metre} defines particle loss. The same source
file, field maps and space-charge settings are used for every algorithm, so the optimization
comparison does not mix changes in numerical fidelity with changes in candidate selection.
Keeping the initial distribution fixed also makes the present search a study of machine settings
for a specified source condition; the separate perturbation assessment changes lattice parameters
while retaining that source distribution.

The retained cathode file has six probe records in addition to the reference and ordinary
particles. Excluding the reference and probes for source characterization gives RMS transverse
sizes of \SI{0.483}{\milli\metre} in both planes and an RMS emission-time width of
\SI{2.551}{\pico\second}. The corresponding Gaussian-equivalent FWHM is
\SI{6.008}{\pico\second}; this conversion does not establish the distribution's functional
form. Ordinary-particle radii extend to \SI{0.9995}{\milli\metre}. Their transverse
momentum moments imply initial normalized RMS emittances of approximately
\SI{0.427}{\micro\metre\radian} in each plane, with nearly monoenergetic
\SI{0.600}{\electronvolt} initial kinetic energy. These are properties measured from the
fixed file, not a verified cathode-temperature model. The original generator recipe and
cathode material are not established by the retained input, so we do not equate this source
with the published study's laser specification merely because their durations are similar.

The injector model builds on the earlier study of Kim et al.~\cite{Kim2024KLSMOGA}, whose
section 4.3 reports \num{10000} macroparticles, 32 radial and at most 32 longitudinal
space-charge cells, and mirror image charges. The present calculation uses the same reported
particle-count scale, cylindrical grid dimensions and image-charge treatment. Its archived
inputs additionally specify a Cartesian \(32^3\) grid and a solver transition at
\SI{5.25}{\metre}; these details are not established by that published description.
For a fixed distribution the calculations are deterministic. The fixed GP observation variance
provides regularization and does not measure tracking-discretization uncertainty.

\subsection{Design variables, objectives and constraints}
The six design variables (table~\ref{tab:variables}) are the solenoid peak field $B_\mathrm{sol}$,
the two quadrupole gradients $G_{q1}$ and $G_{q2}$, the gun phase $\phi_\mathrm{gun}$ and two
common phases, $\phi_\mathrm{acc1/2}$ applied to ACC1 and ACC2 and $\phi_\mathrm{acc3/4}$ applied to
ACC3 and ACC4. Magnetic settings may vary by \SI{\pm50}{\percent} around the
nominal machine setting. The RF inputs are absolute wave phases at the start of tracking because automatic phasing is
switched off~\cite{Floettmann2017Astra}. They are not calibrated off-crest angles. The recorded
gun-phase interval is \SIrange{32.0625}{39.1875}{\degree}; the common ACC1/ACC2 phase spans
\SIrange{-43.4599}{-35.5581}{\degree}, and the common ACC3/ACC4 phase spans
\SIrange{279.0481}{341.0588}{\degree}. The respective nominal phases are
\SI{35.6250}{\degree}, \SI{-39.5090}{\degree} and \SI{310.0534}{\degree}.
Each structure pair receives one common phase, defining the six-control search problem.
These unequal angular windows were inherited from percentage offsets of the stored nominal
coordinates. They define the historical comparison rather than a demonstrated tolerance or an
origin-independent engineering prescription.

represents a different focusing and phasing configuration of the same reference lattice. The
common phase variables are applied to their structure pairs together rather than treated as

The three objectives, all minimized, are the normalized RMS emittances $\enx$ and $\eny$ and the
projected total RMS energy spread $\sigma_E$ at the exit plane,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}) = \bigl(\enx(\mathbf{x}),\ \eny(\mathbf{x}),\ \sigma_E(\mathbf{x})\bigr).
\end{equation}
The energy-spread objective is the RMS energy column of ASTRA's longitudinal statistics,
converted from \si{\kilo\electronvolt} to \si{\electronvolt}; no longitudinal-correlation
subtraction or slice selection is applied. It therefore includes energy chirp. Reducing this
quantity can assist longitudinal matching, but compatibility with the booster also depends on
transport optics and acceptance, which are not simulated here.
For reporting they are expressed in \umrad, \umrad\ and \si{\mega\electronvolt}. The surrogates work with the negated
values, because the acquisition functions are formulated for maximization. An observation is
nondominated if no other observation is at least as good in every objective and strictly better
in at least one. This definition retains settings that improve one emittance or the energy
spread at the expense of another quantity, rather than combining those changes into a single
score. Consequently, coordinate-wise minima describe the ends of the trade-off set and need not
belong to the same accelerator setting.

A setting is \emph{feasible} within the present computational profile if the seven diagnostics
in table~\ref{tab:constraints} satisfy eight inequalities:
\begin{align}
 \sigma_x&\le\SI{1}{\milli\metre}, &\sigma_y&\le\SI{1}{\milli\metre},\\
 \sigma_{x'}&\le\SI{1}{\milli\radian}, &\sigma_{y'}&\le\SI{1}{\milli\radian},\\
 \sigma_z&\le\SI{1}{\milli\metre}, &T&\ge0.90,\\
 E_\mathrm{kin}&\ge\SI{195}{\mega\electronvolt}, &E_\mathrm{kin}&\le\SI{205}{\mega\electronvolt}.
\end{align}
These limits identify the fixed-lattice production configuration. The TDR's optimization
requirements are given separately in table~\ref{tab:constraints}; their source is
Table 2.1.4.6 of the report~\cite{KLSTDR2025}. The production transverse and transmission
limits are looser, while its two-sided energy interval differs from the TDR's lower bound.
The TDR does not justify replacing its requirements with the production limits. The completed
campaigns therefore compare algorithms on the stated computational profile; TDR compatibility
is assessed separately from their archived observations. This is a fixed-lattice study with two
quadrupole controls, rather than a model of the complete TDR linac and downstream matching
system. A valid
simulation that violates any limit is retained as an infeasible observation and cannot enter the
feasible Pareto set, even if its objective values are attractive. Conversely, passing a subset
of diagnostics is insufficient: the classification requires all inequalities to hold together.
This distinction is retained in the constraint-space plots, where the shaded regions apply only
to the displayed coordinates and marker fill communicates the joint constraint status.

The TDR also specifies relative RMS energy spread no greater than \SI{0.5}{\percent}
and geometric emittance no greater than \SI{10}{\nano\metre\radian}; its single-bunch pulse
duration is \SIrange{6}{8}{\pico\second} FWHM (Table 2.1.4.4 and accompanying discussion).
These specifications are distinct from the optimization constraint list. We check the relative
spread as $\sigma_E/E_\mathrm{kin}$, without substituting normalized for geometric emittance
or equating FWHM duration with RMS bunch length.

Reclassifying all archived observations under the TDR optimization constraints gives two
constraint-aware observations and none from either baseline. Both also meet the relative
energy-spread specification. Passing these checks does not establish the remaining pulse,
emittance, stability or downstream matching specifications. The original profile instead
accepts 648, 251 and 200 observations for constraint-aware, unconstrained and scalarized BO.
The difference shows that their original feasible-front comparison describes a broader
computational region than the TDR optimization acceptance region.

As an exploratory sensitivity check, we reclassified the archived observations using published
limits, without changing the original labels. The NAPAC profile~\cite{Park2025NAPAC} requires
transverse sizes below \SI{0.30}{\milli\metre}, divergences below
\SI{0.30}{\milli\radian}, bunch length below \SI{1}{\milli\metre}, the production energy band,
and transmission above \SI{99.99}{\percent}. It accepts 12 constraint-aware observations and
none from either baseline, compared with 648, 251 and 200 under the production profile.
Applying the JKPS size, bunch-length, mean-energy and energy-spread limits~\cite{Kim2025KLSError}
accepts none. These journal checks omit geometric-emittance and ambiguous pulse-duration
conditions rather than substituting normalized emittance or an assumed duration convention.
One constraint-aware observation passes the checked JINST size, energy, relative-spread and
transmission conditions, which is insufficient to certify its full acceptance. Reclassification
describes observed coverage; it does not reproduce optimization under different constraints.

\subsection{Simulation validity and failures}
\label{sec:failures}
Numerical validity and physical feasibility are kept separate. An evaluation is \emph{valid} if
ASTRA completes within its configured time limit, its output can be parsed, all objectives and
diagnostics are finite, and the particle set reaches the exit plane to within \SI{0.1}{\metre}; a beam that
is lost earlier is recorded as a premature loss. Invalid evaluations are never replaced by
artificial penalty values: they are excluded from surrogate training, recorded with their failure
category, charged to the evaluation budget and counted as infeasible. Failed simulations are not
retried, so every budget slot corresponds to exactly one simulation attempt.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Surrogate models}
Each objective is modelled by an independent GP~\cite{Rasmussen2006} with a constant mean and a
scaled Mat\'ern-5/2 kernel with automatic relevance determination over the six inputs, which are
normalized to the unit cube; outputs are centered and, when their observed variance permits, scaled. The models are implemented in
BoTorch~\cite{Balandat2020} and GPyTorch~\cite{Gardner2018}, and the kernel hyperparameters are
fitted by maximizing the exact marginal likelihood. Because ASTRA is deterministic for a fixed
input, the observation noise is not inferred but fixed to
\begin{equation}
  \sigma^2_{\mathrm{obs},m} = \max\bigl(\eta\,\mathrm{Var}(y_m),\ \sigma^2_\mathrm{floor}\bigr),
  \qquad \eta = 10^{-6},\ \sigma^2_\mathrm{floor} = 10^{-24},
\end{equation}
for output $m$, which keeps the kernel matrices well conditioned without smoothing the data. For
constraint-aware optimization, each of the seven diagnostics is modelled by a further GP of the
same form, and all models are combined in one multi-output model with independent outputs.

% C05 insertion after the requested physical-noise equation and model count.
The fixed-noise expression specifies the requested variance in each output's
recorded units. The outcome transform divides it by the square of the observed
standard deviation, and the float64 likelihood additionally enforces a minimum
variance of $10^{-6}$ in the transformed coordinates. Outputs with observed
standard deviation below $10^{-8}$ are centered without variance scaling.
Transmission is constant in the primary data: its requested variance is
$10^{-24}$, its transform scale is one, and its saved effective likelihood
variance is $10^{-6}$. Thus not every channel has unit-variance transformed
observations, and the requested physical variance need not equal the effective
likelihood variance.

There are ten independent GPs in constraint-aware MOBO: three objective
models and seven diagnostic models. The energy diagnostic supplies both energy
inequalities. Across the saved primary models, 1,714 constraint-aware channel
fits completed with L-BFGS and 86 used Adam recovery; the corresponding counts
were 535 and five for unconstrained MOBO, and 180 and zero for scalarized BO.
No saved fit used a restored-prior fallback. These are fitting outcomes rather
than simulation failures or candidate-proposal fallbacks.


\subsection{Acquisition functions}
\label{sec:acquisition}
% C05 replacement for the acquisition subsection's equation and explanation.
% Integrate before the unchanged scalarized-baseline paragraph.
Let $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ be a joint posterior draw at the observed baseline and
at the proposed batch $\mathbf{X}$. Define the feasible sampled baseline
$\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{feas}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_n)$ and the feasible candidate
subset $\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{X})$ by requiring all eight
inequalities at each individual point. The ideal constrained noisy
hypervolume-improvement criterion is
\begin{equation}
 \alpha_{\mathrm{ideal}}(\mathbf{X})=
 \log\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\!\left[
 \mathrm{HVI}\!\left(\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{X})\mid
 \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{feas}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_n),\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{acq}}\right)
 \right].
 \label{eq:qlognehvi}
\end{equation}
This expression defines the ideal target; the implementation uses BoTorch's
qLogNEHVI approximation~\cite{Daulton2021,Ament2023}. It evaluates batch
hypervolume contributions by inclusion--exclusion and applies feasibility
weights to each candidate participating in a contribution, rather than
multiplying the entire batch improvement by one feasibility factor. Sampled
baseline feasibility determines the baseline front. For each sampled candidate,
the weight is $\prod_{k=1}^{8}h(-c_k/\tau_c)$, where $h$ is BoTorch's
fat-tailed sigmoid and $\tau_c=10^{-3}$. Positive improvements and extrema are
also smoothed and combined in log space, with $\tau_{\mathrm{ReLU}}=10^{-6}$
and $\tau_{\max}=10^{-3}$. The unconstrained variant omits the diagnostic
constraints. The separately available analytical Gaussian probability of
feasibility is a diagnostic; it is not substituted for these sampled weights.

The residuals are expressed in their recorded physical units: metres, radians,
electron-volts and transmission fraction, without threshold normalization.
Thus $\tau_c$ corresponds to \SI{1}{\milli\metre},
\SI{1}{\milli\radian}, \SI{0.001}{\electronvolt} and a transmission
fraction of 0.001 in their respective channels. These are scale parameters,
not equal physical transition widths. At a residual of zero the indicator is
0.5; at residuals $-\tau_c$ and $+\tau_c$ it is approximately 0.8089 and
0.1911, respectively. For a \SI{1}{\milli\metre} beam-size limit,
a size of zero therefore receives an indicator of 0.8089, while a size of
\SI{2}{\milli\metre} receives 0.1911. This unit dependence limits the
interpretation of constraint weighting; no residual scaling is changed when
analysing the recorded campaigns.

The executed call sites use baseline pruning and BoTorch's default normal
Sobol samplers: 128 posterior samples for qLogNEHVI and 512 for scalarized
qLogNEI. These counts and the smoothing constants are reconstructed from the
call sites and the matching archived BoTorch version, because acquisition
objects and sampler base draws were not saved. The model dimension and baseline
sizes remain below the Sobol engine's dimension limit. Scalarized qLogNEI uses
$\tau_{\max}=10^{-2}$ and $\tau_{\mathrm{ReLU}}=10^{-6}$. These posterior
sample counts differ from the 1,024 raw candidate-initialization samples.

The weighted-sum baseline fits a single GP to the scalar cost
$g(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_m w_m f_m(\mathbf{x})/s_m$, where $s_m$ are the reporting scales
(\SI{1}{\micro\metre\radian}, \SI{1}{\micro\metre\radian}, \SI{1}{\mega\electronvolt}) and $w_m = 1/3$, and selects batches with the logarithmic
noisy expected improvement (qLogNEI)~\cite{Ament2023}. The scales keep the weighted combination
from being determined simply by the different physical units of emittance and energy spread.
Equal weights define the balanced baseline used in the primary comparison, while the survey
changes those weights to explore other operating preferences. This method does not model the
constraints during candidate selection; the same physical feasibility checks and joint
nondominance calculation are nevertheless applied to its evaluated results, allowing a common
interpretation of the operating points found by all methods.

\subsection{Acquisition optimization}
Batches are optimized jointly with multi-start L-BFGS-B~\cite{Byrd1995}: 1,024 quasi-random
raw samples, 10 restarts and at most 200 iterations. If the optimizer fails or returns an invalid
batch, it is retried with a reduced budget, and as a last resort the batch is drawn from the
campaign's scrambled Sobol sequence~\cite{Owen1998}; every such fallback is recorded with the
proposal and reported. % C05 replacement for the acquisition-reference explanation.
The acquisition reference is recomputed before each MOBO proposal from all
valid observations, including physically infeasible observations. In negated
model coordinates it is
\begin{equation}
 r_{\mathrm{acq},m}=\min_i y_{im}
 -0.05\left(\max_i y_{im}-\min_i y_{im}+10^{-8}\right).
\end{equation}
The additive safeguard is applied numerically in each recorded output unit.
All 360 saved MOBO reference vectors agree with this rule applied to their
saved training sets. The noisy acquisition receives the complete valid
baseline before posterior-based pruning; recorded nominal feasibility is not
used to pre-filter that baseline. This adaptive reference steers acquisition
and remains distinct from the frozen reporting reference.

\section{Simulation study design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Primary comparison}
The three algorithms---constraint-aware qLogNEHVI, unconstrained qLogNEHVI and weighted-sum
qLogNEI---were each evaluated in ten independent runs with fixed seed numbers. Each run has a budget of 160 simulations:
an initial design of 16 points from a scrambled Sobol sequence~\cite{Owen1998}, followed by 18 batches
of $q = 8$. Within each paired run, all algorithms start from the same initial design and differ only
in their acquisition strategy. The protocol (problem
definition, budgets, seeds, reference point and endpoints) was fixed before the simulation comparison; pilot
and screening runs used to validate the pipeline are not part of the reported comparison.

\subsection{Reported quantities and statistical summaries}
\label{sec:endpoints}
The comparison describes feasible nondominated observations and their trade-offs in physical
objective units. Feasible Pareto cardinality is computed jointly in all three objectives and
counts every feasible nondominated measured observation. Additional summaries are the fraction
of feasible evaluations (with all evaluations, including failures, in the denominator), the
number of evaluations until the first feasible point, invalid simulation counts and relative
computational cost. For each algorithm we report the median over runs with a \SI{95}{\percent} percentile
bootstrap confidence interval~\cite{Efron1993} (1,000 resamples with a fixed seed number).
These intervals summarize individual methods; exploratory paired inference is described below.
Neither pooled coverage nor these intervals establish global optimality.
The limitation of the originally designated reporting endpoint is addressed in
section~\ref{sec:discussion}. Constraint-space figures use every numerically valid observation,
including physically infeasible settings, rather than only the Pareto subset. Their limits and
display units are read from each recorded configuration, and the recorded joint feasibility is
checked against those limits before plotting. Green shading marks the allowed interval or
rectangle in the displayed diagnostic coordinates; filled markers satisfy all constraints and
open markers violate at least one, potentially in another panel. Invalid simulations remain
counted in the campaign summary but receive no invented diagnostic coordinates. Individual-run
views and pooled views use the same definitions, with pooled views describing combined coverage
rather than the trajectory of a single campaign.

% C03: post-hoc front-quality analysis; generated from archived stage-04 outcomes.
To assess objective-space quality beyond feasible sampling and set cardinality, we performed
an exploratory analysis of the archived per-run fronts. Objectives were divided by
\SI{1}{\micro\metre\radian}, \SI{1}{\micro\metre\radian} and \SI{1}{\mega\electronvolt}.
A common empirical reference set contained the 53 unique nondominated feasible observations
obtained after pooling all methods and runs. For a measured front $A$ and this reference $R$,
we evaluated the additive epsilon indicator~\cite{Zitzler2003Assessment} and
IGD+~\cite{Ishibuchi2015IGD}:
\begin{align}
I_{\epsilon+}(A,R)&=\max_{r\in R}\min_{a\in A}\max_j(a_j-r_j),\\
\mathrm{IGD}^{+}(A,R)&=|R|^{-1}\sum_{r\in R}\min_{a\in A}\|\max(a-r,0)\|_2,
\end{align}
where the maximum with zero is componentwise. Smaller values are preferred. Exact objective
duplicates were collapsed for set indicators. Empty measured fronts were assigned infinite
distance and zero dominated volume, retaining every evaluation in the budget.


Directional coverage $C(A,B)$ is the fraction of unique vectors in $B$ weakly dominated
by a vector in $A$; both directions are reported. We also examine the across-run probability
of attaining each target $r+\delta\mathbf{1}$, for $r\in R$ and normalized relaxations
$\delta\in\{0.05,0.10,0.25,0.50\}$. These are empirical probabilities of attaining specified
observed trade-offs, with no implied global-front guarantee. Quality histories are evaluated
at full-batch checkpoints, including initialization. Supporting plots and a separate post-hoc
reference-sensitivity analysis appear in appendix~\ref{app:front_quality}.

Exploratory inference used the ten matched groups as the sampling units. Paired bootstrap
intervals resampled complete groups jointly across methods, with 10,000 resamples.
We applied exact two-sided signed-rank tests~\cite{Wilcoxon1945} to four terminal endpoints---feasible fraction,
additive epsilon, IGD+ and the middle post-hoc hypervolume reference---and used Holm correction~\cite{Holm1979}
across all twelve pairwise comparisons. Zero differences were excluded from the signed ranks,
tied absolute differences received midranks, and all sign assignments were enumerated.
The tests assume independent groups and sign-symmetric differences under the null; the
pointwise bootstrap intervals are conditional on the fixed empirical reference and are not
multiplicity-adjusted.


The matched comparison contains constraint-aware qLogNEHVI, unconstrained qLogNEHVI
and equal-weight scalarized qLogNEI. The latter is a specific balanced scalarization,
not a claim to cover all weighted-sum strategies. No constraint-aware scalarized or
budget-matched space-filling arm is included. Consequently the results compare these
implementations but do not isolate a benefit unique to vector-valued acquisition over
constraint-aware scalarization, or establish an advantage over Sobol search. The separate
multiweight survey has a larger aggregate budget and is not substituted for either missing
control. Published MOGA results remain contextual rather than a new matched benchmark.

\subsection{Robustness assessment of operating points}
\label{sec:audit}
To assess sensitivity to machine errors, we select operating points from the predeclared
primary constraint-aware MOBO run. Six representative points are taken from its feasible Pareto set: the minimizer of each objective;
the knee point, closest to the ideal point after min--max normalization of the front; the
balanced point, closest to the normalized front's centroid; and the point of largest crowding
distance~\cite{Deb2002}. A role may select the same setting as another.

Robustness is evaluated with 50 perturbed simulations per representative, drawn with a fixed
seed number. Magnet settings receive independent Gaussian relative errors with standard
deviation \SI{0.1}{\percent}, while the gun and the two common structure-pair phases receive
independent Gaussian errors of \SI{0.1}{\degree}. Within each structure pair the two cavity
phase errors are identical, rather than independent. The same standardized draws are reused
across representatives (common random numbers), while their nominal parameter vectors differ.
Thus six sets of 50 trials produce 300 distinct perturbed settings, not 300 independent trials
of one design. The primary run is the first prescribed member of the primary campaign list,
not a run selected retrospectively for its robustness outcome; the supporting manifest retains
its exact identity. The survey assessment is recorded separately.

For each design we report the observed feasibility fraction and exact Clopper--Pearson
binomial limits, conditional on independent trials from the stated error model. Objective
changes are described by the mean ratio
$D = \frac{1}{3}\sum_m \langle f_m\rangle/f_m^{\mathrm{nom}}$, along with separate
objective means, population standard deviations and empirical fifth/ninety-fifth percentiles
in the supporting data. Signed margins are $(u-y)/|u|$ for an upper threshold and
$(y-l)/|l|$ for a lower threshold. The reported $\mu_{\min}$ is the minimum over all
channels and trials. In particular, the two energy margins use \SI{195}{\mega\electronvolt}
and \SI{205}{\mega\electronvolt} as denominators, not the \SI{5}{\mega\electronvolt}
half-width of the acceptance band. Physical-unit margins are supplied alongside them.
This normalization is a reporting convention and does not define a probability or a
universal measure of tolerance across unlike channels.

The historical composite score is retained in the archived evidence, but is not used to rank
operating settings in the revised paper. Reporting its components avoids conflating sampled
feasibility, relative objective changes and threshold-normalized margins. Source fluctuations,
RF-amplitude errors and alignment errors are outside this assessment. No error-amplitude
scan or comparative robustness superiority over the baseline algorithms is inferred from
these small-jitter tests; expanded error models would be needed for those claims.

\subsection{Operating-point survey}
\label{sec:survey}
To illustrate the operating modes accessible to the injector, we additionally ran single campaigns
(16 initial points and 20 batches of 8, i.e.\ 176 simulations each) of the weighted-sum
method for five weightings $(w_x, w_y, w_E)$---balanced $(\frac13, \frac13, \frac13)$,
high brightness $(0.45, 0.45, 0.10)$, low energy spread $(0.10, 0.10, 0.80)$, horizontal
$(0.60, 0.20, 0.20)$ and vertical $(0.20, 0.60, 0.20)$---and one campaign of each qLogNEHVI
variant. The campaigns use fixed seed numbers; scalarization weight cases have independently
generated initial designs, whereas the two MOBO runs share an initial design.
The five weighted-sum runs together use five times the budget of a qLogNEHVI run, so the
survey is descriptive and is not used to compare algorithms. Its constraint-aware run was assessed under lattice jitter
with the same protocol.

\subsection{Simulation validity and computational comparison}
All 4,800 simulations of the primary comparison were valid; no simulation-invalid failure was
recorded. Physical infeasibility is retained separately: 1,099 evaluations satisfied all
constraints. Weighted-sum BO completed campaigns faster than unconstrained MOBO, which in turn
was faster than constraint-aware MOBO under the same computational setup. GP fitting and
acquisition optimization used double precision on an NVIDIA RTX A5000 GPU, while ASTRA tracking
used CPU worker processes with at most eight concurrent simulations.
All 540 post-initialization
proposals used the primary CUDA tier; none used the recorded CPU or Sobol fallback tier.
Internal optimizer warnings and fitting recovery are retained in the run logs.
For each paired run, campaign duration was divided by that of scalarized BO with the same
initialization. The median ratios are 2.74 for constraint-aware and 1.89 for unconstrained
MOBO. These are measured costs of the recorded implementations and adaptive trajectories,
not intrinsic algorithmic complexity or a GPU-versus-CPU speedup. Campaigns ran sequentially,
with eight evaluation workers per campaign; hardware, software and input identities are
retained in the supporting reproduction manifest.

To avoid double-counting concurrent workers, we also formed the union of recorded evaluation
start/end intervals. The median fraction of campaign time outside those intervals is
\SI{71.4}{\percent}, \SI{57.0}{\percent} and \SI{19.2}{\percent} for constraint-aware,
unconstrained and scalarized BO, respectively. This remainder combines model fitting,
acquisition optimization, scheduling, setup and export; the logs do not support a separate
measured allocation to each component. Summed worker runtimes are retained for reproducibility
but are not interpreted as elapsed fractions.

\subsection{Offline sensitivity of constraint smoothing}
To quantify the unit dependence of the recorded constraint weights, we evaluated single-point
qLogNEHVI on 32 fixed Sobol candidates using frozen early, middle and late surrogate snapshots
from the prescribed constraint-aware run. Each evaluation used 128 common posterior samples
and the same full baseline without pruning, isolating residual scaling from changes in
baseline selection. This diagnostic does not replay the production eight-point optimizer.
Changing the diagnostic representation from metres/radians/electron-volts to
millimetres/milliradians/mega-electron-volts while retaining the raw numerical temperature
changed candidate ranks: Spearman correlations with the original ranking were
0.793, 0.852 and 0.880, with three or four of the original top five candidates retained.

As a unit-invariant sensitivity convention, we divided size, divergence and length residuals
by their upper thresholds, energy residuals by the \SI{5}{\mega\electronvolt} half-band,
and transmission residuals by the allowed loss fraction of 0.1. These scales express distance
relative to the stated production acceptance profile; they are not tuned engineering
uncertainties. At dimensionless temperature 0.01, equivalent unit representations agreed in
log acquisition value within $1.5\times10^{-13}$. Temperatures 0.001 and 0.1 were also
examined as sensitivity settings. The highest-ranked probe candidate remained unchanged in
all cases, while lower ranks varied. No normalized-residual campaign was executed, so these
calculations establish unit dependence and sensitivity, not improved feasible sampling or
beam quality for a corrected method.

\subsection{Predictive checks on unseen batches}
\label{sec:predictive_checks}
We restored all 540 saved pre-proposal GP snapshots and predicted their next eight observations,
verifying that none of these observations appeared in the corresponding training identities.
This chronological check uses \num{1440} post-initialization observations per method and
requires no refitting. Gaussian observation intervals include the saved effective fixed
likelihood variance. Their variance is taken from the posterior covariance diagonal before
any absolute floor imposed by the distribution's variance accessor. The latter can inflate
small-unit uncertainties; both conventions are recorded in the supporting results, but the
covariance-based convention is used here. No production model or proposal is changed.

Nominal \SI{95}{\percent} interval coverage for constraint-aware MOBO is
\SI{78.6}{\percent}, \SI{79.7}{\percent} and \SI{88.8}{\percent} for horizontal
emittance, vertical emittance and energy spread. The corresponding unconstrained values
are \SI{78.1}{\percent}, \SI{81.1}{\percent} and \SI{87.1}{\percent}; the scalarized
cost has \SI{81.3}{\percent} coverage. Thus the saved Gaussian uncertainties are generally
too narrow on the sampled next-batch trajectories. These method-specific adaptive inputs
are not a common independent test set, so predictive errors do not establish a ranking
of surrogate architectures.

For diagnostic observations near a production boundary, coverage ranges from approximately
\SI{80.2}{\percent} to \SI{89.2}{\percent}. The post-hoc neighborhood is within
\SI{10}{\percent} of each absolute threshold, except energy, for which it is within
\SI{0.5}{\mega\electronvolt} of either bound. Counts and physical-unit errors are supplied
in Appendix~\ref{app:predictive}. Transmission is constant; perfect prediction of its
observed value cannot validate discrimination of losses. The previously reported Adam
recovery counts concern fitting completion and do not establish predictive calibration.

\subsection{Interpretation of selected settings}
\label{sec:settings}
Appendix~\ref{app:settings} gives the complete controls and objectives of the six distinct
settings selected from the primary constraint-aware front. The horizontal-emittance extreme
has \SI{2.8061}{\micro\metre\radian} horizontal and \SI{6.8492}{\micro\metre\radian}
vertical emittance, whereas the energy-spread extreme trades larger, nearly equal emittances
for \SI{0.36286}{\mega\electronvolt} spread. These roles describe different compromises
within one selected front; they are not six independently optimized operational modes. No
primary observation exactly matches the nominal input vector, so a measured improvement
relative to that nominal lattice is not claimed.

The quadrupole centers are at \SI{8.70046}{\metre} and \SI{12.50092}{\metre}, each with
\SI{0.065}{\metre} effective length. They lie between ACC2/ACC3 and ACC3/ACC4,
respectively, rather than after the complete linac. For the horizontal-emittance extreme,
the saved normalized emittances are nearly equal near \SI{5.2}{\metre}
(\SI{3.2985}{\micro\metre\radian} and \SI{3.3001}{\micro\metre\radian}), but are
\SI{2.9298}{\micro\metre\radian} and \SI{4.1003}{\micro\metre\radian} near
\SI{12.4}{\metre}. The initially similar source moments and growing downstream asymmetry
are consistent with differing transport through the quadrupole lattice. They do not isolate
quadrupole chromaticity, solenoid coupling, space charge or numerical effects as the cause;
ideal uncoupled linear focusing alone is not an emittance-growth explanation. Such causal
attribution would require controlled diagnostic calculations and is not claimed here.

The energy-spread objective is the projected RMS spread of the full tracked bunch from
ASTRA's longitudinal moment output, including correlated chirp. It is neither a slice spread
nor a dechirped value. The horizontal-emittance extreme has a longitudinal position--energy
correlation of approximately $-0.968$ at the exit, showing that correlation contributes to
its spread. RF phases and upstream focusing vary together across selected points, so their
trade-offs cannot be attributed to a single phase control from these observations alone.
Finally, transmission is unity throughout the primary sample, and the largest mean energy is
\SI{201.41}{\mega\electronvolt}, below the production upper bound. These two inequalities
are unviolated in the observed sample, while each other inequality is violated at least once;
this does not remove any required diagnostic or establish inactivity throughout the domain.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Constraint-aware acquisition produced a larger feasible fraction and a larger feasible Pareto
set over ten runs, while completing campaigns more slowly than the unconstrained and
weighted-sum methods. The exploratory paired analysis supports its feasible-sampling advantage
over both baselines. Evidence for objective quality is more selective: epsilon and IGD+
comparisons with unconstrained MOBO do not pass the chosen multiplicity correction. A larger
feasible set therefore should not be read as uniform superiority of the observed Pareto front. All methods identified feasible nondominated settings,
with the objective projections exposing the trade-offs between transverse emittance and energy spread.
The sampled lattice-jitter tests identify several operating points that remain feasible within
the simulated tolerance model; this does not establish performance on the real machine.

\subsection{Relation to previous KLS injector studies}
\label{sec:previous_kls}
Kim et al.\ optimized RF-cavity geometry and injector beam dynamics with
MOGA~\cite{Kim2024KLSMOGA}. Their abstract reports approximately
\SI{3.79}{\micro\metre\radian} horizontal normalized emittance and
\SI{0.30737}{\mega\electronvolt} energy spread. These summary values differ from the
specific tracked setting in section 4.3, which gives horizontal and vertical normalized
emittances of \SI{3.9586}{\micro\metre\radian} and \SI{3.9424}{\micro\metre\radian},
and \SI{0.165}{\percent} RMS energy spread at \SI{201.79}{\mega\electronvolt}
(approximately \SI{0.3330}{\mega\electronvolt}). The distinction matters when comparing
individual operating points. The present primary knee has
$(\enx,\eny,\sigma_E)=(\SI{2.9983}{\micro\metre\radian},\
\SI{3.5207}{\micro\metre\radian},\ \SI{0.4034}{\mega\electronvolt})$.
It exchanges lower transverse emittances for higher energy spread relative to that tracked
example. Different acceptance limits and settings prevent attributing this exchange solely to
the algorithm. The earlier source was specified by laser parameters, whereas the present study
reuses one fixed distribution file; equality of their full initial distributions is not established.

The NAPAC comparison~\cite{Park2025NAPAC} used independently varied cavity phases and
normalized fractional energy spread, while this study uses paired cavity controls and absolute
RMS energy spread. Its feasible-region and weighting conventions also differ. Consequently,
those published results supply prior-work context rather than a matched-budget control for
this study. No new MOGA/NSGA-II campaign or reevaluation of its published setting is included,
and no MOGA speedup or superiority is inferred from the present comparisons.

The subsequent JKPS study~\cite{Kim2025KLSError} reports nominal energy spreads of
\SIrange{0.34145}{0.48248}{\mega\electronvolt} for its selected cases. Its tabulated emittances are geometric, whereas
ours are normalized, so those emittance values are not directly compared. Its beam-size and
bunch-length limits were \SI{0.2}{\milli\metre} and \SI{0.9}{\milli\metre}, respectively, compared with \SI{1.0}{\milli\metre} for both here;
JKPS also imposed a \SI{0.5}{\mega\electronvolt} energy-spread limit, whereas here energy spread is an objective
without an upper feasibility cutoff. Feasibility therefore has different meanings across the studies.

The JKPS operating-error model used RF-gradient and phase standard deviations of \SI{0.2}{\percent} and
\SI{0.2}{\degree}, with \SI{0.1}{\percent} magnet-strength errors. It also studied \SI{100}{\micro\metre}
position errors and \SI{0.4}{\milli\radian} rotations, using 1,000 samples per stage with a Gaussian distribution
truncated at two standard deviations. Our tests use \SI{0.1}{\degree} RF-phase and \SI{0.1}{\percent} magnet-strength
errors with 50 samples per representative, omitting RF-gradient and alignment errors. Thus the
observed feasibility under lattice jitter complements the earlier tolerance study; it does not
show greater robustness under its broader error model. The earlier study identified alignment
errors as a principal sensitivity, supporting their inclusion in future assessments.

\subsection{Limitations}
The empirical Pareto sets characterize the settings observed within the sampled design domain
and do not establish global optimality. Their extent depends on both the finite evaluation
budget and the selected beam-quality limits, which were fixed before the campaigns. All results
use one injector model and a fixed source distribution, with finite macroparticle and grid
resolution. The cited JINST settings establish partial correspondence with the earlier model,
but the inspected source supplies no particle/grid refinement error bound or full solver-transition
equivalence. Absolute numerical uncertainty relative to the method differences and constraint
margins is therefore not quantified here, even though all methods use common tracking settings. The perturbation tests exclude source fluctuations, RF-gradient errors
and alignment errors, so the retained feasibility applies to the stated lattice-jitter model.
The earlier JKPS results motivate extending this assessment to the omitted error channels.
Ten repeated runs provide an estimate of variation with initialization, rather than a high-powered
statistical ranking, and no comparison with accelerator measurements is made. These limitations
define the scope in which the observed feasible trade-offs and relative sampling behavior can
be interpreted.

The next-batch checks also show undercoverage of the saved Gaussian intervals; estimated
constraint probabilities must therefore not be interpreted as calibrated machine-reliability
probabilities.

The RF-domain interpretation is another limitation. The earlier JINST paper reports phases
relative to maximum energy gain~\cite{Kim2024KLSMOGA}, whereas the present inputs are absolute
wave phases; those earlier selected settings do not calibrate the current crest. In a post-hoc
partition of the ACC3/ACC4 interval, its upper quarter
(\SIrange{325.5561}{341.0588}{\degree}) contains 259, 345 and 394 sampled observations for
constraint-aware, unconstrained and scalarized BO, respectively, all outside the energy band.
This observation identifies unproductive sampled coverage, but upstream settings vary jointly.
It neither establishes a causal phase cutoff nor predicts algorithm rankings after changing
bounds. Physical crest calibration and an engineering basis for the unequal phase windows
remain to be established.

The originally designated fixed-reference hypervolume endpoint was uninformative: it was zero
for all campaigns because the reporting reference $(\SI{1.5}{\micro\metre\radian},\ \SI{1.5}{\micro\metre\radian},\ \SI{1.5}{\mega\electronvolt})$
had emittance components below the observed values. This does not indicate an absence of feasible
solutions. We therefore omit the original endpoint from the comparative figures and tables.
The exploratory reference-sensitivity analysis in appendix~\ref{app:front_quality} uses new,
explicitly post-hoc conventions and cannot retrospectively replace that endpoint. The original
records and reference remain unchanged.

%==========================================================================================
\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
We studied constraint-aware batch multi-objective Bayesian optimization for a six-parameter,
three-objective, seven-constraint design problem of a \SI{200}{\mega\electronvolt} electron injector linac for KLS, in a paired,
budget-matched comparison over ten runs with failure-inclusive accounting. All simulations were
valid, and each method identified feasible nondominated solutions. The pooled feasible fronts
contained 49, 27 and 17 observations for the constraint-aware, unconstrained and weighted-sum
methods, respectively; these are empirical trade-offs, rather than certified globally optimal
fronts. Constraint-aware acquisition had a median
feasible fraction of \SI{40.6}{\percent}, compared with \SI{15.0}{\percent} and \SI{12.5}{\percent} for the two baselines. All sampled
lattice perturbations were feasible, with source jitter explicitly excluded. Paired analysis
supports improved feasible sampling under the stated profile, while evidence for objective-space
quality depends on the comparator and endpoint. The recorded data and analysis scripts support
regeneration of these results.
Future work will extend the robustness analysis to source fluctuations with
a validated distribution generator, distribute the simulations across compute nodes, and test the
optimized settings on the machine.
